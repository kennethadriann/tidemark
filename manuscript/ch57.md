## Chapter 57 — The Stair

Morning down there wasn't a light so much as a thinning, the dark going from can't-see to can-just, and the yards started itself up from the low end, a pot, a scrape, a man coughing like the cough was work. Maning slept through the front of it. He'd bought the sleep off a bad drop and I wasn't going to spend it for him.

I'd meant to lie there and think. I was good at lying there and thinking. It had carried me across three weeks of cold country and down to the bottom of the world and it had set me down exactly here, at a fire gone gray with a man asleep beside it who'd left a thing where I'd have to step over it. So I lay there and did the thinking, and it went the same round it had gone all night, the same stone with the same two sides, and somewhere in the going-nowhere my body got tired of waiting on my head and sat itself up.

Then I was on my feet. It hadn't come with a click. There was no moment in it I could point at and say, there, that was the choosing. My knees did it before the rest of me signed off, feet running out ahead of the head because the head, left in charge of the matter, would still be sitting down.

I stood a while getting my legs to believe it. The old itch came up, the one that wants to make a job of a thing so the job can be worked at instead of done, sort what to carry, run the way in my head, turn a leaving into a morning's chores I could lose myself in and never quite finish. I felt it come and I let it lie. There wasn't much to sort. You carry less going up than going down. The thing I was taking up the wet way was myself, which was the whole trouble, and a man can't pack that and can't leave it home.

Maning was awake. I don't know for how long. The hat was still over his face but the breathing had gone from the deep even haul of a sleeping man to the shallow kind that's listening. He'd have heard me stand. A man who reads a load in his sleep hears a friend get up.

"Going up, then," he said, from under the hat. It wasn't a question, and he didn't move the hat to ask it.

"Going up," I said.

He was quiet a moment. "Wet way's marked to the third lamp. Past that it's dry cut-offs and they'll be full of backs. Stay in the crowd. A man alone up there is a thing people look at. A man in a line of backs is nobody."

"You could tell me to sit down," I said. "Wait for a better day to do it."

He took the hat off his face then and looked at me with the one eye the fire lit. "I could. I've watched you wait for a better day since the tier. Never once saw one turn up." He put the hat back down over his face. "Go on, before you talk your feet out from under you."

So I went. I didn't say anything back, because there wasn't a thing to say that wouldn't have been me standing there saying it instead of going, and he knew that better than I did, which was why he'd put the hat back.

I went up out of the yards the way I'd come down into them, past the poor edge fires and the men just starting to be men again in the thin light, and not one of them looked. A man walking toward the up-way at first light is the dullest thing a yards has to offer. Backs go up. That is the trade. I was one more going, and being one more was the last of the cover I'd have for a good while, so I wore it while it lasted.

The slope took my feet and the fires went small behind me and a bend took them, and the sound of the place thinned out the same as it had thinned when I came in, only run backward. I was walking into the quiet again. It wasn't the same quiet. Coming down, the quiet ahead had been empty and cold and nobody's, and the thing at the far end of it had been Maning. Going up, the quiet was only the last of the quiet. Somewhere past it the country filled itself back in, the lamps drew close and then closer, the ways forked into places with people standing in them, and up past all of that, where the signal ran thick and a wrist meant a thing again, there was a file with my name on it and a number kept inside it, and the number would have me the moment I stood where it could see.

I didn't hurry at it. A man doesn't hurry at the one thing he's spent a life stepping around. But I didn't stop, and the not-stopping was the whole of what I'd brought. I'd learned on the black water weeks back that the only piece of any of this I could call my own was the part where I didn't turn around, and here it was handed back to me, the same small use, going the other way. Put a foot up the stone. Then the next one. Leave the turning-around alone.

The wet way started where it always had, a shine of cold water climbing into the dark, and I put my legs into the low end of it. It was as cold as the first time and I was going up it now instead of down, which the water took no notice of. Behind me the yards was a warm smudge going out one fire at a time as the light came up and the men let the night burn down. I didn't look back to count them. I kept to the marked stone and I climbed.

*End of Chapter 57.*
