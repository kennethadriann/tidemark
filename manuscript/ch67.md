## Chapter 67 — The Warm Part

Here's the honest version of the next three days, because you don't need the long one and I don't want to walk it twice.

Down. That's the whole trip. Down the freight tiers in the reverse order I'd climbed them, which sounds like it ought to be the easy direction and is not — going down you carry your own weight out ahead of you the entire way, and my knees had opinions about that they'd apparently been saving up for a special occasion. I slept where the lamps went thin. I spent the last of my metal on food and then I went without. The country got poorer and wetter the lower I dropped, the way it always does, the world running its one dependable trick: the closer you get to the bottom, the more it costs to be down there and the less anybody's got to pay with.

I passed backs going up and backs going down and didn't talk to any of them. A month ago I'd have had a line ready for every one, a tired-man-looking-for-work line polished to a shine. I didn't bother now. Turns out the best disguise I ever found for being nobody was actually being tired. I'd finally stopped acting it.

I'll skip to the water. The water is where it turned back into something.

---

The last leg home isn't a stair. It's a swim.

You come to a place where the floor of the old world just quits, goes under, and the only way on is down through a gap in a wall into the black. I'd done it once going the other way, the first night of all this, with Sol ahead of me telling me I'd said I could swim. I remembered the cold the way you remember a dentist — not the feeling, just that you didn't care for it. Then I was in it, and the feeling came back in installments, the way it did the first time, cold arriving in the fingers, then the chest, then somewhere behind the eyes, each one a separate little bill.

Somewhere in the middle of that black, my wrist went out.

I felt it happen. The number I'd climbed a month to get back, the one I'd put on my own arm on purpose at the top of a freight stair — it dimmed, flickered, quit. Under the line the signal frays, and the count can't reach down here to hold a figure on your skin. A month of walking to become countable, and I'd just swum back out of the count in about forty strokes.

And here's the thing I noticed, treading water in the dark with my lungs starting to ask questions. I didn't mind.

I'd minded going the other way. Going out I'd stood on the stair and done arithmetic about every step spending my anonymity, the dark wrist that would mean the wrong thing up top. Coming home the dark wrist meant I was nearly there. Down here the number was never the thing that said whether I got to exist. Down here a woman looks at your face.

I came up out of the water into the drowned mall, the one with the parking sign somebody once filed a complaint about, back when complaining about parking was a thing a person could spend a day on. I lay on the tilted floor and breathed and dripped. Then I got up, because the smell had already found me.

Lugaw. Rice and ginger, coming off a stove somebody had jury-rigged out of a dead electrical riser, the most ordinary smell in the world and it went through me like the cold hadn't. I followed it the way you follow it when you're seven, which is apparently the age you revert to the second you're close to a pot that's yours.

---

The kitchen hadn't moved. That was the first thing, and it shouldn't have landed as hard as it did. Of course it hadn't moved. But I'd carried the picture of it up a month of dark and worn the edges off it, and here was the real one, warmer and louder and more cluttered than the one in my head, with actual socks on the actual line between the two pillars that used to hold up a department store.

Tessie saw me first. She was at the pot, where she is, and she looked up and took in the whole of me — wet, thinner, a month older, standing in the doorway like a thing the sea had decided it didn't want after all.

"You're dripping on the floor," she said.

"I missed you too, Tessie."

"Sit." And there it was, the word the way she says it, where it isn't a suggestion and there's no version of the evening where you don't sit. A bowl I didn't ask for was already coming at me. I sat.

Then the rest of them came up around me the way a room does when a thing it half-stopped expecting walks in. Lim first, drifting over with his tuneless hum, patting my shoulder like we'd last spoken yesterday — which, to him, we more or less had. Pia looked up from her bottle caps, decided I was less interesting than the caps, and went back to them, which is the warmest welcome that girl has in her and I took it as the honor it was.

And Sol came and stood over me with her arms crossed and read me.

That's the thing Sol does. She read the month off me the way I read a file — not the words, the weight. The wet. The thin. The way I was sitting, which was like a man who'd forgotten chairs. She didn't ask where I'd been or whether I'd found him. She already had most of it off my face, and she knew better than to make a man unpack a month before he's had something hot.

"You went all the way down," she said. Not a question. "And you came all the way back."

"Both ways," I said. "On my feet. It's a long story and the ending's fine."

She looked at me a second longer, and I knew she'd caught the thing I wasn't saying — that a man doesn't walk back out of the deep carrying nothing, that I had something lashed to my back heavier than my sack, and that the ending being *fine* was doing a lot of quiet lifting in that sentence. Maning, dated out. The tense turned on my own file. A sentence that missed. All of it still up there in the dark where I'd left the man it belonged to, and all of it coming home with me whether I said it tonight or not.

I didn't say it tonight. I'm not proud of that and I'm not sorry either. I'd been gone a month, and the lugaw had fried garlic on top that somebody had bothered to make, and I wanted one warm hour before I handed the room the thing that would change the temperature in it. The telling would keep till morning. The warm wouldn't.

So I ate. The garlic, the steam, the pot the size of a child. Lim hummed. Pia sorted her caps by a system only she understood. Nobody looked at my wrist, and this time there was nothing on it to look at, and for the length of one bowl that felt less like hiding and more like being let in.

The warm had wanted me to stay, back at the start. I'd always been careful around things that wanted me to stay. I set the careful down by the door with my wet sack and I stayed anyway.

*End of Chapter 67.*
