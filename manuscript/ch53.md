## Chapter 53 — The Number I Put Down

He didn't say anything else about it. He pulled the boot on and lay back with his hat over his face like a man who's done talking, and after a while his breathing went long and even, and I was left with the fire and the job he'd handed me.

Go get the number.

It sounded like an errand. Go get water, go get wood, the kind of sentence you can put your boots to. I lay there and looked at the underside of it and there was no floor. Getting a thing means there's a place the thing is. My number wasn't in a place. That was the whole trouble with it.

I'm a Reader. Reading is looking at a thing that's in front of you and telling true what it says. Give me a wall of slates, a file, a wrist, and I'll tell you what's written on it and mostly what's written under it. But a Reader is no use pointed at a thing that isn't there, and my number wasn't there. My wrist had been dark since the black water under the mall. You can't read a dark wrist. There's nothing on it to read. It's just skin.

So not reading. Remembering. I'd had the number once, clean, back when the wrist still told me things and I was a man with a tier and a ration and a dull safe life I was very good at. I reached back for it. You know the reach. The one you make for a thing you're sure of, the year something happened, the price a thing used to be. And it came up soft.

Not gone. I want to be honest about that, because I'd told Maning down was softer and not gone and I'd better hold myself to the same coin. It wasn't gone. I could find the shape of where it had been. Small. It fit on a wrist. It had felt, when I first read it, like a thing put in the wrong tense, but that's a different story. The number itself, the actual count of it, had gone the way of a song you've stopped singing. You know there were words. You couldn't stand up and give them to anybody.

And here's the part I didn't much like, lying there in a stranger's yards with Maning asleep two feet off. I didn't lose it. I put it down.

Somewhere up the line, walking toward his date, I'd made a quiet trade with myself and never called a meeting about it. I'll count his, I'll not count mine. One number is all a man can carry and stay walking, and his had a face on it and mine had only me, and I've never been the thing I'd walk three weeks to save. So I'd closed the book on my own and pocketed the key and got so good at not looking that the looking-place had gone cold and quiet, like a room you stop heating.

It was, I'll say it, a tidy thing to do. A number that only ever goes down, and a man who doesn't care for a column with no tap at the top of it, so he draws a line under it and turns the page and calls it peace. I knew the move. I'd made it a hundred times on worse things than myself, back when tidy was the whole of what I was for. I felt the shape of that and set it down too, because it was late and it wasn't the job. The job was the number.

There was one place it surely still sat, whole and current. Up in the count, in my file, where the machine kept it for me. It had never once lost track of the thing I'd let go soft. That's the joke. The score minded my number better than I did, kept it clean and dated and ready, same as it keeps everybody's. But that was the one shelf I couldn't reach up and take it down off of. Reaching for your own file is standing up in a crowd and waving your dark arm over your head. I'd spent three weeks learning to be a wrist nobody read. I wasn't going to unlearn it to check a date.

All right, I thought. If you can't read it and you can't remember it, you count back to it. That's arithmetic. A Reader can always do arithmetic. Start from the day it went dark, add the days you've walked, take it off the thing you started with, and there's your answer, cold but yours.

Except I hadn't kept the days.

That was the second floor to go out from under me that night. The walk had no days in it. It had lamps. It had a wet crossing and a fork that breathed and a fire I warmed my hands at and a man passing through and another one after him. I could put those in order, I could tell you which came before which, but I couldn't put a number of dawns to any of it, because up the long run there are no dawns, just dark and less dark, and I'd stopped counting sleeps somewhere past the top stop, the same as I'd quit the other thing. I'd thrown away the tally that would have let me build the number from the outside. I'd been thorough. It's the one thing I've always been.

So there was no reading it, no remembering it, and no counting my way back to it. Three doors and a wall behind each. Maning wanted the thing I'd stopped looking at, and the trouble wasn't that it hurt to look. The trouble was I'd taken such good care that there was nothing left in the looking-place to see.

I lay there and did the one thing I've always been able to do, which is nothing, patiently, in the dark. It got me no number. Waiting is fine for a man coming up a wet way with a light. It's no good at all against a thing you buried yourself.

The fire sank. Maning breathed slow under his hat. Two fires over, the one with no tune had given it up at last and gone to sleep. I'd have to go at it in the light, I decided, whatever going at it turned out to mean, and I hadn't the faintest idea yet, and I lay and looked at the dark until it was a little less dark.

*End of Chapter 53.*
