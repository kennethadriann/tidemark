## Chapter 56 — What He Came Back To

He came back off the drop after dark, later than he'd told me, which with Maning meant it had gone worse than the man who set it wanted known. I heard him before the light of him reached the fire, the slow scuff of a back that has carried too long and is telling its own feet it's nearly there. Then he came into the edge of the fire and got the empty carry-frame off his shoulders and stood it against the wall, careful, the way you'd set down a full one, because the shoulders don't believe the load is gone for a good while yet.

He sat. He worked one knee and then the other. For a stretch he didn't say anything, and I didn't talk either. Partly to leave a tired man his quiet. Mostly because a man who isn't talking isn't asking.

I'd spent the afternoon laying in things to say. Not answers. I didn't have an answer. Just shapes to set in the air where an answer would go, so it wouldn't be empty when he looked at it. I'm good at that. It's most of what I'm good at.

"Bad drop?" I said, because somebody had to spend the first word and it might as well be a cheap one.

"Water sat wrong." He held his hands to the fire. "Line was good. The young one on it was not." He turned a palm over and looked at a rope-mark on it like it belonged to somebody else. "Talks a fine drop. Knows the knots better than me, I'll give him that. Then you put him at the wet lip with a real weight behind him and something in him locks. Stands there telling you why the line won't hold. Line held fine. It was him that wouldn't go on it."

"So you went on it."

"So I went on it." He said it with no weight, a plain fact about his day. "That's the job. Somebody's got to be the one steps on the bad-looking line to show the line's not the problem."

He dug in his coat and came out with a fold of leaf, cold rice and something oily inside it, and tore it and gave me the bigger half without offering it first, which is how Maning hands you a thing, done before you can say no. We ate. The fish in it had been strong before it ever went cold, and I said so, and he said a beggar at the bottom of the world doesn't send the plate back, and that was the nearest either of us came to a joke all night, and it was enough.

I kept waiting for the question. All afternoon I'd built the shapes for it and now I sat holding them, waiting for the slot they went in. Well? You go up and get it. Did you find your number, Adz. It didn't come. He ate his cold rice and rubbed the sore knee and looked at the fire, and he did not ask me the one thing he'd sent me off to do.

At first I took it for kindness, a tired man putting a hard talk off till morning the same as he'd done that first night. Then I sat with it a little longer and it wasn't that. A man who wants the answer and is only too tired to hear it tonight still asks. He can't help it. It leaks out of him sideways even when he means to hold it, are we set, did it go, is the thing done. None of that was leaking off Maning. He wasn't sitting on a held question. He didn't ask whether I'd gone up and got the number because the number was never what he'd sent me for, and I'd worked that much out on my own in the afternoon. It is one thing to work a thing out alone at a fire. It is another to watch the man not-ask and see it proved on his tired face.

And he'd told me the rest of it too, without meaning to, back when he was talking about the young one on the line. You don't learn a man from how he talks the work. You learn him at the wet lip, when the weight's on and the next step is the one he doesn't want, and you see whether he goes or stands there explaining the rope. He'd said it about a body up a bad drop, somebody I'd never lay eyes on. He hadn't pointed it at me. He didn't need to point it. It was only how he saw men, all of them, the ones on his lines and the one across his fire, and I happened to be sitting in the middle of it.

So there it was, said plain by a man who didn't know he was saying it to me. The number was never the errand. I was the errand. He'd sent me up a stair I was afraid of and gone off to work a drop, and he'd come back not to hear a figure but to find out, over cold fish, whether the man who'd walked three weeks to warn him would spend one step of his own being seen. He'd find out the same way he found out about the young one. Not by asking. By watching.

"You eat that or just look at it," he said, meaning my half, and I ate it. Then he stretched out with the hat over his face like the night before. He didn't say we're not done with this. He didn't say a word about the morning. He'd set the question down where I'd have to walk past it, and then he'd lain down next to it and gone to sleep, easy, a man who could wait longer than I could. He'd proved that twice now. He'd likely prove it again.

I fed the fire. He hadn't asked, and the not-asking had told me more than a night of asking would have, and none of it had moved my feet an inch toward the stair. I sat with that. The rice was gone and the fire was low and up in the count my number sat where it sat, minding itself the same as ever. I put another stick on and watched it take.

*End of Chapter 56.*
