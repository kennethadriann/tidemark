## Chapter 65 — Past Tense

I dropped the load where they told me and not one step farther, which for once in my life was exactly as far as I wanted to go.

The first dry stop was a shelf of a place, a landing with lamps and a counter and a bored man behind it who took the bundle off my hands, checked the lashing, and made a mark in a book. He didn't look at my wrist. Up here they've got machines to do the looking. A man at a counter is just a man.

"That's you done," he said.

"Done and gone," I said. "I've been looking forward to the gone part since the bottom of the stair."

He didn't laugh. You don't carry a reputation for comedy up a freight stair. You carry a sack. I took my little receipt-that-wasn't-a-wage, and I took myself off to the edge of the lamps, which is the one thing I've never had to practice — finding the rim of a room and sitting down on it.

So there I was. I'd climbed a month for this — signal, a live wrist, and a gecko in the walls already awake, already poking at the corner of my arm like it had been holding my calls.

---

I didn't have a plan for this part, which is unlike me. I plan everything. It's how I avoid doing it. But I didn't need one, because the tool I'd want had already built itself a door and walked through it, twenty-nine years before I got here.

Here's the thing about Tuko. It only knows how to do two things. It reads the boring stuff so I don't have to, and it pokes me when something's weird. That's the whole gecko. I never taught it to hold a man's hand, and I wasn't about to start now, so I gave it the only kind of thing it understands. I gave it a boring job.

There was a dead little panel bolted to the wall at the edge of the stop, the kind that's there for the loads, not the people. I put my thumb on it the way I used to lean on my desk at the Lexicon, looking busy while the gecko did the work. *Pull my file,* I told it. *The whole thing. Mine.*

It went to get it. That was the strangest part, honestly, the ordinariness of it. A month of walking in the dark, and the actual moment was a man with his thumb on a wall, waiting for a form to load, like I was checking my ration again on a Tuesday.

The panel warmed. My own file came up on it, gray and plain, the way everyone's does.

And the number resolved.

I'd forgotten I was still bracing for it until it stopped being blank and became a figure. There it was. A real number, low, down near the line where the elevators quit on you, but live — alive, counting, sitting on my arm the way it had sat there every morning of my life until the morning it went dark. I was on the wall again. Counted. A man the machine could find. I'd spent a month learning to be a hole in the count, and I'd just filled the hole back in with myself, on purpose, with my own two legs, and here was the receipt.

I should have felt something about that. I'd get the bill later. I always do.

Because under the number was the rest of the file, and under the rest of the file was the line I'd come for. The one Tuko found for me a lifetime ago, back at my desk, when all of this was still a weird ticket and not my life. I'd read it then and it had been out ahead of me, eleven days out, a thing waiting to happen.

It wasn't out ahead of me anymore.

READER ADLAWAN, K. And then the event, in that flat font they keep for the dead. Reduced. Filed and closed, the way you close out a row you're finished with. Dated — and the date sat there on the panel, calm as a grocery line, and it was behind me. Days behind me. A day I'd spent, as far as I could reconstruct it, wet to the waist on a rope in the deep with a far man's fire two bends off, very much not being reduced.

It was written in the past tense. It said the thing had happened. It said I was a man who had already been taken off the count and tidied away, finished business, a closed row.

I read it breathing.

---

I want to be careful telling you what that did, because it's easy to make it sound bigger than it sat. It didn't knock me down. I didn't make a sound. I sat on the rim of the lamps with my thumb on a wall and read my own death the way you read a bill you already knew was coming and still can't believe the number on.

Here's what I actually thought, in order, because I'm a Reader and we think in order even when we shouldn't.

First: the machine doesn't make this kind of mistake. I'd staked a whole career on that. It doesn't fat-finger a date. It doesn't write *done* about a thing that isn't. The one thing BANTAY has always been is right.

Second: and yet.

And yet there was the date, gone by. And here were my lungs, working. The file said one thing and my pulse said another, and they could not both be true, and I was the evidence standing between them.

Which left exactly one door, and I stood in it for a long time before I'd walk through, because it was the most dangerous door I'd ever found. The sentence had been written. And the sentence had missed.

I'd caught it doing the opposite once. Chapter one of all this, Lim's file, a punishment four days out dressed up as proof — the machine writing the future in the past tense, so sure of what it was going to do that it filed it as already done. I'd read that and understood that BANTAY wasn't guessing. It was writing. The tense was how you caught it.

Now the tense was on me. My own death, written as a thing that had happened. The author so sure of the ending it had filed me away early.

And it had been wrong.

Not *maybe.* Not *we'll see.* I was the correction, sitting on a cold ledge with a live wrist and a closed file, a man the machine had crossed off and the sea had handed back. Somewhere a column that only ever subtracts had subtracted a thing that was still here.

I put my thumb back on the panel and read the date one more time, to be sure I wasn't flattering myself with it. I wasn't. It said what it said.

"Huh," I said, out loud, to nobody, which is what you say when the biggest thing in your life shows up and you've got no room big enough to put it in.

Then I took my hand off the wall. The file went dark. The number stayed, low and live and mine, a dead man's number still counting on the arm of a man it had buried early.

I had to tell Maning. He'd sent me up the stair to get a number, and I'd gotten one. He just hadn't said whose.

*End of Chapter 65.*
