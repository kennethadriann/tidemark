## Chapter 64 — Boss

I sat with it the way I sit with everything, which is to say I sat with it until it stopped being a wall and turned into a ticket.

That's the one thing nobody tells you about panic. Panic is just a problem you haven't filed yet. A wall is a thing that stops you. A ticket is a thing with a field in it marked *how.* As long as I looked straight at the two facts — man reads wrists, my wrist is dark — they sat across from each other and refused to move, and that's a wall. So I quit looking at them straight and looked at the room instead.

Here's what the room told me. People went up one way, through the bright mouth with the line that didn't want to admit it was a line. But the field wasn't only people. It was loads. And the loads didn't climb all this way up to the top stop just to lie here and look pretty. They came up to go on up — into the tiers, where the dry folks lived who needed the deep junk and never once wondered whose back had carried it. Water goes up. Salvage goes up. Somebody carries it.

And a load doesn't have a wrist.

I watched the up-end for a while to be sure I wasn't just talking myself into something, which is a hobby of mine. I wasn't. The people-line fed the bright mouth. But off to the side of it, on a steeper, meaner-looking stair, parcels were moving — a man backing into a lashed bundle and standing it up, a string of them going up into the dark one after another, nobody taking anybody's arm. The checker didn't even turn his head. You don't read a sack. You weigh it.

So the move was obvious, and I hated it, which is usually how you know a move is right. I couldn't go up as a man. I could go up as a thing a man was carrying.

---

The up-end had its own still man, the way the receiving heap had its tally-man. Every place that matters down here has one — somebody who does nothing but decide, and gets paid for the deciding. I used to be one. I know the breed. He was sorting backs onto up-loads, pointing with two fingers, and I went and stood where he'd see me and let him finish being busy before I opened my mouth.

"I'll carry," I said, when he got to me. "Up."

He looked at me the way they all look at a new back — down the arms first, pricing the shoulders, then the face, last and least. "Dark wrist," he said. Not a question. Everybody could see it now; I'd stopped bothering to hide it down here where it hadn't mattered.

"Dead," I said. "Has been a while."

He didn't care about that, which was the whole point of the freight way. He cared whether I'd drop his load. "You carried before?"

"Came up from the yards. Light one. Dropped it at the heap, whole." I tipped my head back toward the receiving slope, toward the still man down there who'd carved me my one line. "Ask him. He'll tell you I'm the one dropped nothing."

It was the only coin I had, and I'd spent half of Chapter 63 pretending it was worthless — a reputation built on not screwing up a single bag. Turns out a man whose whole trade is handing loads to strangers will pay real money for *this one won't lose it.* He looked at me another second, decided I was more boring than trouble, which is the nicest thing anyone's ever decided about me, and jerked two fingers at a bundle near the foot of the mean stair.

"That one. It goes to the first dry stop and no farther. You don't go farther either." He said the last part flat, and I understood it was the part that mattered. Backs went up with the loads and backs came back down. Nobody wandered off into the tiers. "Water's at the stair-lamps. Drop it where they tell you. Then you're done and you're gone."

"Done and gone," I said. "My favorite kind of job."

---

I'm not going to walk you up the stair. It was steep and it was long and the load leaned on my bad side the whole way, the way they always find your bad side, like they can smell it. I did the work. The thing I want to tell you about isn't the work. It's what happened to the air.

I'd felt this before and hoped I'd misremembered it. Going down, somewhere past the yards, the sense of being watched had lifted off the back of my neck like a hand taken away, and I hadn't known how heavy the hand was until it was gone. Now, going up, it came back on. Not all at once. A draft of it first, high up the stair, the faint wrong feeling of a room you thought was empty. Then more. By the third stair-lamp it was a flat palm laid on the back of my head again, pressing, patient, and I knew without being told that I'd crossed back into the thick country, where the count reaches down close and reads everything with its lips moving.

I kept climbing, because the alternative was to stand on a stair in the dark and have a feeling at it, and I had a load to drop.

And then my wrist woke up.

I felt it before I saw it — a warmth under the skin where there'd been nothing for a month, the small private heat of a thing switching on. I almost dropped the bundle. I got it against the wall and I turned my arm over and there it was, glowing faint up through the skin the way it had glowed every morning of my life until the morning it didn't. Alive. Reaching. I watched it the way you watch a man you buried sit up.

I waited for the number. You have to understand what I was waiting for. One number would tell me the thing I'd walked a month not to learn — whether the sentence they wrote me had come due and missed, or whether I was a dead man who hadn't been told yet. I stood on a stair in the thick dark with a stranger's load against the wall and I waited for my own arithmetic to come home.

The number didn't come. The wrist glowed and hunted and the number stayed a blank, the way a panel stalls when it's been offline too long and doesn't trust its own memory yet. And then, into the blank, something else came up instead. Not the dead-people font. Not a sentence. Three small letters, low and familiar, in the corner of my own arm, from the one thing in all that machinery that had ever been on my side.

*boss.*

I stood there and read it about four times. Tuko. My gecko in the walls, the lazy little script I'd built to do my job so I could look busy, still running after all this time, still living where it had no business living — and the second my wrist came back in range it had found me, the way it used to poke me at my desk when something was weird. Only now the weird thing it had found was me.

I'd spent a month learning to be a hole in the count. I'd just walked myself back into range on my own two legs and handed the count the hole to fill. On purpose. Because the only copy of my number that could tell me if I was alive was up here where it could read me back, and you don't get to have the answer without standing where the question can see you.

I put my hand over the glow, out of old habit, the way you cup a flame. It didn't go out. Of course it didn't. I picked the load back up.

"Yeah," I said, out loud, on a dark stair, to a line of text on my own skin. "It's me. I'm back."

*End of Chapter 64.*
