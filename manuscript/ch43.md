## Chapter 43 — The Middle of the Yards

Sitting still is a thing I'm good at, and I don't say it to run myself down. A man who's good at sitting still is good at watching, because they're the same trick done with the eyes open. I'd spent a day and a night turning myself into part of the poor fire so that nobody would wonder what I was doing there, and now that nobody wondered, I could get on with the real work, which was looking at the middle of the yards and learning how it ran.

The middle was where the trade was. From my corner I could take it in without turning my head much, which was the whole good of the corner. There were the better fires, deeper in, the ones with men around them who had something to swap, and past those, back against the far wall where the black water didn't reach, a long low shelf of dry stone with a lot of coming and going to it. That was where the loads landed. I'd worked out that much the first night without trying. What I set myself to now was the rest of it.

You'd think a place like that would be noise and mess and no order to it, and from a distance that's how it looked. But nothing men do for a living is really a mess once you sit with it long enough, and I had nothing to do but sit with it. So I watched a load come in, and then another, and by the third or fourth I started to see the shape under the coming and going.

There were men down there who didn't carry. That was the first thing. Everybody else moved, backs going up loaded and coming down empty, the empty ones standing around blowing on their hands and waiting to be sent up again. But there were two or three who mostly stayed put, and the moving happened around them the way water moves around a post. When a load came onto the shelf, it was one of these still men who went to it, walked the length of it, put his eye on it, and then said a thing I couldn't hear that turned a heap of freight into so many backs' worth of parcels going so many ways. I knew the sort. I'd met one two lamps down and been taken on by him on the meanest terms he had. Down here they'd be the same animal, only more of them, because more came through.

I gave the biggest part of a day to just telling those men apart from the rest, and it was slow, and it was the kind of slow I don't mind. You can't pick a decider out by his size or his clothes, because a man who decides looks like any other tired man until something needs deciding. You pick him out by what he does with his stillness. A carrier resting is a man storing up for the next load, and you can see it on him, how he's only half in the yards and half already up the slope. A decider standing still is all the way there, taking the whole shelf in at once, keeping a count in his head of who's up and who's down and what's owed and what's coming. I know that stillness better than I'd like to. I watched it move across three different men over the course of the day and I marked them, quiet, like you'd mark a snag in a channel you meant to cross later.

Nobody minded me doing it. That was the gift of the day before. A man with no place gets looked at when he looks, but a man who keeps a poor fire and warms the backs coming off the cold water has a reason to be sitting where he sits, and a man with a reason can stare all he likes. I fed the fire when it wanted feeding so the staring would keep its cover, and I watched the middle, and by the low end of the day I could have told you, if you'd asked, which three men down there turned freight into work and which way most of it went. Up, mostly. The yards took the deep stuff and sent it up the ordinary ways to the lit country, a little at a time, on the backs of men who'd never see where it started or where it ended, only their own stretch of it. Same as any trade. Same as me, once, moving numbers I never owned from one column to another and calling it a day's work.

What I wanted to see I didn't see, not that day. I wanted to see how the yards behaved when one of the far men was about to come in heavy, the surfacing the old man had told me about, the long-gone kind coming back through with a load too big for a fresh back. I didn't know yet what that looked like, which was the trouble with knowing it was coming and not knowing its face. So I watched for the shape of a waiting, like a man watches a still pool for the first push of a thing moving under it.

And near the end, when the racket had thinned and the fires had sunk and men were bedding down against the walls, I thought maybe I caught the edge of it. One of the deciders, the leanest of the three, wasn't sending all his empty backs up. He had four or five men he kept down, kept close, wouldn't put on the ordinary loads going up even when there was room for them. They grumbled about it, a little, the way men grumble at work held back from them, and he let them grumble and kept them down anyway. Idle backs a decider won't spend are backs he's saving for something he knows is coming. I didn't know it was my something. It could have been any heavy drop off any far man the yards was owed. But it was the first thing I'd seen down there that pointed forward instead of past, and I sat with it a long while after the fires were low, keeping my own low, not sending my one thought up any ordinary way either. Holding it back. Watching the pool.

Nobody came in. The backs stayed held and the man who held them lay down among them, and the yards went as quiet as it went, which wasn't very. I fed the fire once more and put my shoulders against the cold wall and watched the middle go dark by fires, one and then another, and I stayed awake in it longer than I needed to, for no reason I could have paid for, and then I slept.

*End of Chapter 43.*
