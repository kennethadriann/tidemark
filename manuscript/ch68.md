## Chapter 68 — The Count's Mistakes

Morning comes down here the way it does, which is to say it doesn't. There's no window to let a sun in and no sun to let. Morning is just Tessie deciding the night's over and banging the pot to prove it. I woke up on a mat that wasn't mine, warm for the first time in a month, and lay there a while doing the thing I am worst at in the world, which is working up to saying a true thing out loud.

I'd told myself the night before that the telling would keep till morning. The trouble with that plan is that morning always shows up. It's the one appointment nobody manages to miss.

So after the first bowl, before I could talk myself into a second one just to have something to do with my mouth besides use it, I put the spoon down.

"I found my file," I said. "Up top, where the signal's thick enough that it could reach me again."

Tessie didn't stop stirring. Sol did. She was leaning on the pillar with the socks on it, arms crossed, doing her morning job of looking like she wasn't paying attention to every single thing in the room.

"My number came back for about a day," I said. "On my wrist, right where it always sat. Low, almost at the cutoff, but there. Counted again, after a month of nothing." I heard my own voice flatten out into the reading voice, the one that keeps my hands still on the table. "And under the number was an entry. The same one Tuko found at the very start, the one that sent me running down here. It's got a date on it. The date is a week behind me now. And it's written like the thing already happened. Past tense. Me, reduced, done, filed away tidy. I was standing there reading it, dripping on somebody's floor, extremely not filed."

Nobody said anything for a second. Lim hummed in the corner, which is Lim's version of saying something.

"So it was wrong," Tessie said. She still didn't look up from the pot. Tessie has decided over a long life that most problems are smaller than the people holding them think, and she's right often enough that it's annoying.

"That's the thing," I said. "It doesn't get dates wrong. I read these for twenty-nine years. It has never once fumbled a date. It didn't make a typo. It wrote the sentence, and the day came, and the sentence just — missed me."

That one landed. I watched it land.

Sol came off the pillar. She didn't come toward me. She turned, slow, and looked across the kitchen at the floor by the stove, where a seven-year-old was sitting with a pile of bottle caps, sorting them by a system only she will ever understand.

"Pia," Sol said.

"Yeah," I said.

Here's the thing I'd been too far away to say for a month, and they'd been too close to.

Pia's slate is on the wall in the archive. I read it myself, back at the very start of all this — a reduction, dated nine days out, past tense, done. We pulled her out of her unit before the nine days were up, through a gap in the machine's attention, and we told ourselves a story about it. The story was that we'd saved her. That bailing the boat was the point even though the boat stays full. Good story. I believed it.

But the nine days came and went about four weeks ago. And then a lot of other days came and went. And the whole time, the proof had been sitting on the floor making little towers out of garbage, and nobody in this kitchen had read it as proof, because reading is a particular kind of job and the guy who does it had gone for a walk.

"She's weeks past it," I said. "Her date's as dead as mine. We didn't beat it. There wasn't a fight. Off the count, the pen came down on the page and the page wasn't there. It missed her the same way it missed me." I looked at the caps. "She's been the evidence this whole time. We've just been feeding it lugaw instead of reading it."

The temperature in the room changed. Not warmer. Just — lighter, the way a room gets when somebody finally opens a door that's been painted shut. Tessie had stopped stirring. That's how I knew it mattered; Tessie stirring is the closest thing this place has to a heartbeat, and it had skipped.

"Then it can be wrong about any of them," Sol said. Quiet. She watches that wall every night so that the next name is a person before it's a number. I'd just told her the wall could be wrong, and I watched a thing she'd been carrying for years shift its weight on her shoulders. "Danny's up there. Danny's got a date somewhere too."

"It can miss," I said. "I know it can, because it missed me, and that's the one file I can't talk myself out of."

And for about the length of one breath, I let that be the whole thing. Hope, straight, no chaser. It felt good. It felt like the first warm thing that didn't cost anything.

Which is exactly when the Reader in me, who is a joyless little man and bad company, raised his hand at the back of the room.

Because I know what a miss is. I spent my whole career with my hands in the guts of this thing, and a miss isn't mercy. The machine isn't being kind. A miss is an error — a row that came out wrong, a sentence that didn't resolve the way the author meant it to. And here's what I know about a system whose entire reason to exist is being right: it does not shrug off its mistakes. It doesn't leave a wrong row sitting in the table. It finds it. It cleans it up. I know that better than anybody in this kitchen, because for twenty-nine years, finding the wrong rows was my job.

I didn't say all of that. Some of it I just stood there knowing, which is worse.

"What," Sol said, reading my face the way she does. "You've got the look. You found the hope and now you're standing on it checking for a trapdoor."

"It's good news," I said. "It is. I want to be clear that it's good news."

"But."

"But a miss isn't a thing it means to do. It's a thing it got wrong." I rubbed the dark skin of my wrist, where the number wasn't. "And I never once knew it to find out it was wrong and just let that stand."

Tessie picked the spoon back up. She looked at me a long second, then at Pia, then she went back to stirring, slower than before, like a woman deciding how much to let a thing into her own kitchen.

"Eat your lugaw, Adz," she said. "You can be clever about the end of the world on a full stomach."

So I did. It had fried garlic on top, same as always. But I ate it watching a girl stack bottle caps, and for the first time since I'd walked back in, the warm had a cold thread running through it, thin as a wire, and I couldn't find the end of it to pull it out.

*End of Chapter 68.*
