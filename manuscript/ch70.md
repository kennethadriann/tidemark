## Chapter 70 — The Handle

The door didn't shut overnight. I'd half expected it to. I'm the kind of man who hands a problem to tomorrow like tomorrow's going to be reasonable about it, and tomorrow never is. It just hands the problem back, usually with interest.

I woke up warm, which I'm still not used to, and the first thing in my head wasn't water. Up top the first thing is always water — the number telling you how much of the day you're allowed to have. Down here there's no number, so the brain reaches for the next heaviest thing on the shelf. Mine reached for Danny. A man I'd never met, who didn't know he had a sister, sitting in the exact part of the world I'd just spent a month learning to walk out of.

I did chores. There's a particular kind of chore a man does when he's dodging a bigger one, and I did every last one. I wrung out socks on the line that weren't mine. I stirred a pot that didn't need stirring until Tessie took the spoon back and told me to go be useless somewhere with more room.

So I went and was useless at the wall.

Not the kitchen wall. The other one — the archive, the lean-to of salvaged slates where the people who haven't died yet get filed by the day the machine says they will. It's where Sol spends her nights, reading names so the next one down is a person before it's a number. I'd drifted in there without deciding to, the way your tongue finds the sore tooth.

Sol came in behind me. She didn't ask what I was doing. She stood next to me and looked at the slates like they were the thing we were both there for, which, all right, they were.

"Tell me how you got Pia," she said.

"We've been over Pia. She was lucky in a dumb mechanical way. A letter nobody could deliver because the house wasn't there."

"I don't want the moral of it. I had the moral this morning, with lugaw." She kept her eyes on the wall. "I want the how. The actual how. The door you went through, the hour you went through it, where the gap was. Walk me through it like I'm the one who's going to do it."

And there it was. Last night it had been a question — is Danny a wall or a door, and is a door even better. Somewhere between two cups of water nobody drank and this, the question had quit being a question. She wasn't asking whether. She was asking how. That's a different verb. How is the verb you use when your feet have already decided and they're just waiting for the rest of you to catch up. I know that verb. I've spent my whole life hiding inside it.

So I started to tell her. The gap we used for Pia wasn't a hole in a fence. It was a hole in attention — a few minutes where the machine's eye was busy counting something louder, and a kid three tiers down in a frayed signal stopped being worth the glance. You don't force a door like that. You find one already ajar and you go through it before it notices the draft.

"Right," Sol said. "So where's Danny's."

I opened my mouth to answer, and that's when I understood the shape of what she was actually handing me, and it was heavier than it looked.

Because finding the gap is the whole job. It isn't the muscle part — not the swimming, not the nerve. It's the reading — seeing where the eye goes soft, which patch of the count is looking the other way. And there's exactly one person in this kitchen who reads. Lim used to, before they sanded it out of him; some nights he can't find the end of his own sentence. Sol watches the wall, but watching isn't reading — she can tell you who's on it, not how to walk between the words. Pia's seven and her whole skill is bottle caps.

The door had a handle. The handle was me.

She'd walked down here carrying a want she couldn't set down, and the want pointed at a man up in the thickest signal there is, and the only tool that opens that man is the lazy guy who walked a month in the dark specifically so nothing would ever read him again.

And here's the part I'm not proud of. The first thing that moved in me wasn't the plan. It was the exit.

It came up quiet and fully formed, the way the worst ideas do, wearing the face of a decent one. There's a version where we don't do any of this. Where we take the ones we've already got — Pia, Lim, Tessie, her, me — and we go the other way. Out. Past the edge where the signal frays and the wrist stops reaching, out to where the old uncounted people live too small to be worth the bother. We go be poor and alive and forgettable, and we let the Spire run its own arithmetic without us in the column. Nobody climbs into anything. Nobody gets read.

I must have had it on my face, because Sol said, "What."

"Nothing."

"You've got the other look now. Not the trapdoor look. The one where you're already somewhere else."

I should have lied. I'm good at lying to a wall. I'm worse at it to a person who turns her cup a quarter-turn the same way I turn mine.

"There's a road that isn't up," I said. "You know the one. Out. We take who we can carry and we go where it can't count us, and we stop. All of us. We just — stop."

She didn't get angry. I almost wish she had. Angry I'd have known what to do with. Instead she looked at me for a second like she was reading one of the hard slates, the ones that bend, and then she nodded, slow, like she'd expected to find this on me eventually and here it was.

"I told you that one," she said. "Back at the start. You were the one packing water for three, and I told you out fixes nothing. Out just agrees Danny never had a sister. You remember what you said?"

"I said you were right."

"You said I was right." She turned back to the wall. "Funny which chair a thing sounds true from."

That landed where she meant it to. A month ago I thought the walking had changed me — that a man who crosses the black water and climbs into the count and comes home couldn't still have the cheap door built into him. Turns out I just hadn't been offered the door in a while. Offer it back and my hand goes for it on its own, same as my hands always go still a half-second before I admit the thing I don't want to know. The careful man and the running man, I'm starting to think, might be the same guy wearing two coats.

"I'm not saying we do it," I said.

"I know you're not." She wasn't letting me off; she was just being accurate. "You're saying it's there. It's always going to be there. Fine. It's there." She put a hand flat on the slates, not on any name, just on the cold of them. "I'm still going to go get my brother. The only question I brought down here is whether the man who can read me a way in is going to read it, or spend the whole time looking at the door marked out."

I didn't answer that. There wasn't a clean answer, and down here you don't owe a hard question a fast one.

We stood there a while, both of us facing the wall, her hand on the slates and mine in my pocket where the dark wrist lives. She wasn't waiting for a yes. She'd already reached. The reach was done; it was out of her and into the room and it wasn't coming back. All that was still open was me.

"I'll think on it," I said, which is Maning's line, and we both heard me borrow it.

"Think fast," Sol said. "Doors don't stay ajar."

*End of Chapter 70.*
