## Chapter 50 — Dated Out

I told him about the wall first, because he had to have the wall before I could set him on it.

The slates, I said. The ones he'd hauled down the line for years and handed over for a bowl and never thought about again, the way you don't think about the rock you sell to a man building a step. They weren't junk. Down in the kitchen we hung them on a wall and read them, and they weren't lists of anything he'd have guessed. They were people. A name, and a date set against the name, and the date was the day that person was going to be dead.

Maning didn't say anything to that. He sat with his forearms still on his knees and his eyes on me, level, waiting to see the whole shape of the thing before he decided how to stand under it. He was good at that with a load. I watched him do it with this.

"Written like it already happened," I said. "That's the part I can't shrink down for you. It's not somebody guessing at a man. It's put down done, finished, in the past, before the man's gone and done it. And then he does it. Because it's written."

"You read that," he said. Not quite a question. Checking the footing.

"I read all of them. That's the job I took. Nobody told me to. I just couldn't stop once I'd seen the first one."

He turned that over. A far salvager knows better than most that the score reaches for what it's already written down, that going out past where it can see you is the only place a man breathes easy. He'd told me as much himself, a long time back, in the kitchen, before any of this. So he wasn't sitting there thinking I'd lost my mind. He was sitting there doing the arithmetic of why I'd carried a wall's worth of talk three weeks down into the wet to lay it in front of him in particular.

He got there about the same time I said it.

"There was one came down with your loads," I said. "Not this trip. Back a ways. I read it because I read all of them. Your name was on it, Maning. Dated out."

I'd said it. It didn't feel like anything to have said it. I'd built the saying of it up into a large thing over a lot of cold miles, and now it was just four or five plain words sitting in the smoke, and he had them.

He looked at me for a moment. Then he looked back at the coals.

"My name," he said.

"Your name."

"There's other Manings."

"There's not many. And there's not many that a slate follows down the line from up where you work, into the yards you'd surface at." I kept my voice flat, because if I dressed it up he'd hear the dressing and stop listening to the thing under it. "I looked for the reasons it wasn't you. I'm good at finding those. There wasn't one I could stand on."

He didn't answer that straight off. He picked his cup back up off the ground between his boots, found it empty, and held it anyway. He wanted the cup in his hands, I think, empty or not.

"A man's going to die, Adz," he said finally. He said it mild, almost kind, like he was letting me off a hook I'd hung myself on. "That's not news you walk to the bottom of the world to bring. I could've told you that much for free, and you a boy. Everybody on your wall's dated out. It's called being born."

"That's what I told myself the first time I read one," I said. "Then I watched one come true on the day it said, on a girl who'd done nothing to earn the day but be near the wrong knife. It's not the ordinary kind of dying, Maning. It's the machine's kind. It writes the day, and the day comes, and it comes soon."

"Soon," he said.

"Soon." I made myself hold his eye. "Not old age. Not out on the far work with your boots on when you're seventy. Days. Weeks at the outside. It read you thin already, and you are thin, you said it yourself last night."

That reached him where the rest hadn't. Not the wall or the past tense or any of the strangeness of it. His own body. He looked down at his own wrist, dark and quiet like every wrist down here, and then at the backs of his own hands, which had gone a little bony this last while out, and I saw him weigh what I'd said against what his knees had been telling him in the cold all morning, and not like the sum.

He was quiet a good while then. The yards went on around us, loud and busy and not caring, backs going to the shelf and away from it, the whole widening waking into its work the same as it did every morning it had ever had.

"Huh," he said, at last. That was all. Just the one small sound a man makes setting a heavy thing down on the ground to look at it.

He didn't come apart. I hadn't thought he would, and I'd have not known what to do with it if he had. He wasn't a coming-apart kind of man. He took bad news like he took a load he hadn't asked for, shifting it around until he found where on his back it would ride without putting him over. He turned the empty cup around once more in his fingers and set it down again, careful, in the exact spot it had been.

"You're sure," he said.

"I'm sure it's written. I'm not sure of anything past that. That's why I came."

"To tell me."

"To tell you. And to see if there was more than telling."

He nodded slowly at that, not agreeing to anything, just marking that he'd heard it and would get to it. He wasn't there yet. He was still a step or two back, sitting with the plain fact of it, a man who'd carried his own death down on his own back for a bowl of something hot and never once felt the weight of it.

"Well," he said. He rubbed a hand over his face, once, and left it at that. "That's a hell of a thing to bring a man in the morning."

"I know it."

"In the light, I said." He almost smiled, and didn't. "Should've let you keep your complicated."

He put another stick on the fire, because it had sunk while I talked, and we sat and watched it catch.

*End of Chapter 50.*
