## Chapter 54 — The Only Place It's Kept

I didn't sleep much, and what I got wasn't worth the name. When the yards started up again, that low general noise of men who'd decided it was day because the loads didn't care either way, Maning was already awake. He'd got the fire back from its ashes and had water going in the black cup, and he was working a knuckle like the cold had put something in it overnight.

He looked at me and read the night off my face the way he'd read a load.

"You didn't get it," he said.

"No."

He didn't seem surprised, and he didn't make me feel it. He poured, and handed the cup across, and I took it and held it for the warmth more than the water. He waited. He was good at that in a way I'd always thought was mine.

I gave it to him plain, because there was no clever way to hand a man three empty hands. I told him I'd gone at it every way a Reader has. I couldn't read it, the wrist being dark and a dark wrist being just skin. I couldn't remember it, having set it down myself somewhere up the line and gone so long not looking that the place I'd kept it had gone cold. And I couldn't count my way back to it, because I'd walked three weeks by lamps and not by days, and thrown out the tally that would've built the number from the outside.

He took each of those in turn, lifting each one to feel where its weight sat.

"So it's nowhere," he said.

"I didn't say that." I turned the cup. "It's one place."

"Go on."

"Up in the count. My file. Where the score keeps it." I said it flat, because it was a flat thing, and the flatness of it was the worst part. "It never lost it. That's the joke of the whole business. I let mine go soft as a stopped song and the machine's got it filed clean and dated and current, same as it's got yours, same as it's got everybody's. The one number I can't lay a hand to is sitting on the one shelf that never forgets."

Maning thought about that. He was a man who liked a thing to be somewhere. A load is somewhere. A crossing-line is somewhere. He'd spent a life going out to where things were and bringing them back, and here I was telling him the thing he wanted was in a place, a real place, filed and kept.

"Then you go and read it," he said.

"That's the cost."

"What cost. You said it's kept. Kept means it's there to be read."

"Kept up in the count," I said. "Reading my own file means going up where the count can read me back. It's not a shelf you sneak to. Asking the score what it's got written down on me is the same as standing up in the middle of everybody and putting a dark arm over my head and waving it. Here. This one. The wrist you've all been not-reading for three weeks. Have a look."

He went still, and I watched him get it, because he was quick and it was a simple ugly thing once you saw the shape of it.

"You'd light yourself up," he said.

"To read the number that tells me how long I've got, I have to walk back into the exact thing the number's counting down. It's the one errand where the going and the getting are the same move. I can't fetch it quiet. There's no quiet version. The looking is the being-seen." I drank a little of the water, which had gone from too hot to nearly right. "You told me to go get my number before you'd say yes or no. I've spent a night finding out that going to get it costs me the number and then some. I'd know my date about the same minute the count started writing me a fresh one."

He didn't answer straight off. He picked up a bit of broken slat and fed it to the fire and watched it take, which is a thing a man does with his hands when his head's gone somewhere the hands can't help. I let him have it. I'd had all night; he could have a minute.

And I'll be honest about the small crawling thing that went through me while he sat there, because I said I'd hold myself to my own coin. A part of me was glad. Not glad. Relieved, the small mean relief a lazy man gets when the job turns out to be locked and he can set it down and say, well, I tried the door. I knew that relief. I'd felt it a hundred times over a column I didn't want to add up, back when finding a reason not to look was the same skill as looking, and I'd told myself both were being careful. I felt it try to stand up in me now, dressed as good sense, and I made myself see it standing there before I let it sit back down. Whether it was mine or something I'd been handed to keep me quiet, I still couldn't tell, and I didn't chase it, because it wasn't the job either.

"So the number's real," Maning said at last, slow, "and it's kept, and the getting of it is the one thing you walked three weeks not to do."

"That's the size of it."

"Huh." He turned the boot over that he hadn't put on yet and knocked it on the stone out of habit, empty, nothing in it. "You've made your whole hard trip and got to the door and the door's the trap."

"About."

He set the boot down and looked at me across the low flame, and he didn't say what he'd decided, because he hadn't, and I hadn't given him the thing he'd said he needed to decide by. We were two dark wrists at a fire with a number between us that only the machine could read out loud, and neither of us was fool enough to go ask it. He put his hand out for the cup and I gave it back, and he finished what was in it, and for a while we just sat with the fire and the noise of the yards going about its day around two men who weren't going anywhere yet.

*End of Chapter 54.*
