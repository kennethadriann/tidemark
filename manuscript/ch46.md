## Chapter 46 — What Came Up the Wet Way

The light took a long time to stop being a light and start being a man. Heavy going is slow going, and whoever carried the lamp was carrying more than the lamp, so it came on at the pace a loaded man comes on, which is no pace at all if you're the one watching and want it done. It would climb into view low on the water, and then the way would bend and take it, and then it would come back a little bigger and a little nearer, and each time it came back I told myself I could see the shape behind it now, and each time I couldn't, not really. It was still just a cold light on the wet, coming in.

The yards had gone a particular kind of quiet by then. Not silent. A working quiet, the sound of a lot of men holding still on purpose and a few men getting ready to not hold still at all. The held backs were down by the mouth, spread the way the decider had set them, and the decider stood at the near end of the cleared shelf with his weight even on both feet and his hands loose, which is how a man stands when he's about to have work and knows exactly what the work is. I stayed at my poor fire and fed it a stick it didn't need, so my hands would have a reason to be doing something other than nothing.

Then the light came up out of the last bend for good, and there was the load.

I saw the load before I saw the man, the way you're meant to down here, the same as everybody there saw it. It rode high and back on somebody, a dark squared-off weight lashed the careful way, not the fast way, a thing packed by a man who'd had it on his back a long while and meant to keep it whole. It came up out of the wet at the mouth and the whole shelf-end of the yards leaned an inch toward it without a foot moving. A couple of the held men waded down to meet it. There was a word or two thrown, low, the flat short words men use when a thing is heavy and everybody already knows the plan. "Easy." "Left of you." Nothing you'd write down. The kind of talking that's really just hands, said out loud.

They got under it with him and walked it up onto the cleared stone and set it down, and setting it down took four men and him and a slow count, and when it was down the man who'd carried it stood up out from under it.

That was when I saw him instead of the load.

He straightened slow, a hand going to the small of his back where a weight carried too long always goes, and he stood there in the lamp-wash getting the load's shape out of his own body, rolling one shoulder and then the other. I couldn't have told you his face yet. He was turned three-quarters off from me and the light was behind him and there was a fire's worth of smoke between us. But I didn't need the face. I had the rest of him first, and the rest of him is the part that doesn't lie, because a man can fix his face for you but he can't fix how his own tiredness sits on him.

I knew the walk before I let myself know the man. He took a step off the shelf and it was a careful step, a heel set down and tested before the weight went onto it, and I'd seen that step come down into the kitchen a hundred times over a hundred loads of somebody else's junk and somebody else's bad news. I knew the roll of the shoulder. I knew how he set his hand to the stone shelf as he came off it, giving it a small pat like you'd pat the flank of an animal that had carried you a long way, which was a thing he did to loads and to doorframes and to the sides of boats and once, that I'd seen, to the top of Pia's head. It was Maning. He'd come up out of the wet at the bottom of the world with a stranger's load on his back, and he was standing thirty feet from me working the ache out of his shoulders, and he had no idea I was there.

I didn't get up.

Everything in me had come up onto its feet inside my chest, but I kept the rest of me sitting at the poor fire with a stick in my hand, because getting up was still the wrong thing and knowing it was him didn't make it the right thing. He was a far man surfacing with a big drop, and the deciders' whole attention was on him and the load, and the last thing that quiet needed was a worn stranger crossing the light toward the one man everyone was watching. There'd be a time. This wasn't it. This was only the seeing, and I'd walked a long way for just the seeing, so I made myself take it and not spend it.

He got the load handed off. The decider came down the shelf to him and they stood close over the squared-off weight and talked in the short way, the man pointing at a corner of it and Maning shaking his head once and saying something back I couldn't hear, the ordinary haggle of who-carries-what-up that has nothing to do with anybody's death and everything to do with a day's pay. He looked thinner than I remembered. Longer out will do that. But he stood there arguing a load like a man with weeks of walking still in his legs and no notion at all that his own name was down on a slate in the dark, dated, waiting for him to come back through and collect it.

I fed the fire. I watched him be alive and not know the shape of it, the way I'd watched other men not know things, the same as I'd once not known things myself. He finished with the decider and stepped back and put his hand to his back again and looked around the yards for the first time, not for anybody, just the look a man gives a place he's landed in, and his eye went across my fire and across me and didn't stop, because there was nothing at my fire worth stopping on. A worn man warming his hands. I let his eye go past. Then I looked back down at the fire, so that when he looked again, if he looked again, there'd still be nothing there.

*End of Chapter 46.*
