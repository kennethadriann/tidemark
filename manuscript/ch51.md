## Chapter 51 — More Than Telling

We watched the stick catch and for a while that was the whole of it. A man can look into a fire a long time and call it thinking, and both of us did, and neither of us was in a hurry to be first to call it something else.

Then Maning said, "So." He said it the way he'd set one load down to get his hands under the next. "You came to tell me, and to see if there was more than telling. You told me. Now there's the more."

I'd known it was coming. I'd walked three weeks toward this exact question and I still didn't have it folded up neat anywhere I could just hand it over.

"Maybe," I said. "There's a maybe."

"A maybe."

"It's the best I've got. I'm not going to sell you better than I have."

He nodded at that, like it was the first thing I'd said all morning he didn't have to check for weight. "All right. Give me the maybe."

So I gave it to him the plain way, because he'd have heard it if I tried anything else. Down where I'd come from, I said, under the water, in the kitchen, the writing doesn't hold on a man as tight. The wrist goes dark and stays dark. The score reaches for you and comes up a little short. I'd seen a man down there hum a song out of a hole where the song should've been trimmed clean, and it came back on him because nobody up top was minding him close enough to keep taking it. Off the count, I said. The further off you get, the softer the thing sits.

"Softer," he said.

"Softer. Not gone. I won't tell you gone. I don't know gone."

He turned that over slow, from more than one side. He picked up the empty cup again and looked into it like there might be an answer in the bottom of it that hadn't been there the last three times.

"You know where I work," he said.

"Out. Past everything."

"Past everything. Where the wrist quits reaching altogether and a man breathes easy." He set the cup down. "You're telling me to go down to get off the count. I go out to get off the count. It's my whole trade, Adz. I've been doing the thing you walked three weeks to teach me since before you could carry your own water."

That landed, because it was true, and because I hadn't a clean answer to it standing ready. I sat with it a second so he'd see I wasn't going to talk over a good point.

"You go out," I said. "And you come back. Every time. That's the difference I've got. Out you visit. You go, you fill your back, you turn around, and you come up the wet way into the yards where it can see you fine, to sell what you carried to a man who's on the count same as you. You've never once stayed gone. The place I'm talking about, you stay. You don't surface to sell. You just don't come back up where it can find you."

He didn't say anything to that for a bit. The yards went on around us, a decider calling two backs onto a fresh load down the shelf, the ordinary morning noise of a place that had no idea two men at a middle fire were deciding anything.

"Stay," he said at last, and I heard everything he'd hung on the one word. He was a man with a fire he fed and a stretch of far ground he knew better than the backs of his own thinned hands and a way of living that had nobody's thumb on it, or so he'd always had it. And I was asking him to walk away from all of it into a hole under the sea on the say-so of a slate.

"I know what I'm asking," I said.

"I don't think you do. But go on. Say the rest of it."

"There isn't much rest. Come down with me. The kitchen's real, it's warm, there's people in it who look at your face instead of your wrist, and it's off the count as far off as I've found. I can't promise you the date won't come. I can only tell you it comes slower and softer the further you get from where it wrote you. That's the whole of the maybe. I'd rather you had a slow soft maybe than a fast sure nothing."

He almost smiled again, like he had over the complicated the night before, and again he didn't spend it.

"You walked three weeks to tell a settled man to quit his ground and go live at the bottom of the world," he said. "You. The laziest article ever to hold a spoon."

"I've noticed. I'm not over it."

"No." He looked at the fire, and then out at the yards, at the shelf and the backs and the wet mouth he'd come up out of the night before, taking a long unhurried inventory of everything I was asking him to set down. He was not a man you rushed at a fork, I knew that much about him. He'd stand a breath at the mouth that breathed cold and read it before he put a foot wrong.

"I'm not saying yes," he said.

"I didn't ask you to say yes this morning."

"Good. Because I'm not." He picked a splinter off the stick he'd fed the fire and turned it in his fingers. "A man doesn't pull up a life he built with his own back because a friend came a long way and looks sorry about it. Even a friend who's usually right. Maybe especially him." He flicked the splinter into the coals. "I'll think on it."

"That's all I came for. The thinking."

"No it isn't," he said, mild. "You came for me to say yes. But the thinking's what you'll get for now, so I'll let you have it back like it was the plan."

He was right, and there was no profit in arguing a man off a thing he'd read correct. I let him have the last word on it, and we sat, and the fire he'd fed took proper hold at last and put a little more light between us, and for that while neither of us reached for anything more.

*End of Chapter 51.*
