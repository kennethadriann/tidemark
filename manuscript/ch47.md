## Chapter 47 — Across the Light

I waited for the yards to stop looking at him.

That took a while. A heavy drop is a thing the whole place has an interest in, and even after the load was down and handed off there was a tail of attention on it, men drifting past the shelf to see the size of what had come in, the decider's people breaking it into what would go up and how. Maning stood in the middle of that a good stretch, answering it, being the man who'd brought the thing, and I sat at my fire and let him be that man. A stranger doesn't walk up to the center of a thing while the thing is still the center. You wait for the edges to come back.

They came back like they always do, slow and without anybody deciding it. The load got broken and carried off in pieces. The held men, spent at last, drifted toward their own fires with the loose walk of men whose waiting was over. The lean decider's stillness went ordinary again, his eyes off Maning and back on the general run of the place. And Maning himself, the work of arriving done, got smaller in the yards' eye, like a man does once the interesting thing he carried is no longer on his back. He'd stopped being the drop. He was just a far back now, tired, looking for a fire and a meal like anybody.

He found one two along from the shelf, a middle fire with men already at it, and lowered himself down at the edge of it with the care of a man whose knees had opinions. Somebody handed him something hot. He took it in both hands and sat over it and didn't talk, which I understood. There's a tiredness that only arrives after you've put the load down, and it doesn't want conversation. It wants to sit and hold something warm and be nobody a while.

I gave him that while. Then I got up.

I'd thought, sitting there, about how to do it, and every clever way I came up with was worse than the plain one. I could catch his eye from off and let him come to me. I could get near and wait to be noticed. But a man being watched and worked out from the dark is a man who reaches for his knife, even a friend, and I didn't want the first thing Maning felt to be a stranger's attention crawling up his neck. So I did the plain thing. I walked over like you'd walk to a fire you meant to sit at, not fast, not soft, my hands where he could see them, and I came into his light from the front, where a man can watch you come.

He clocked me the way he'd clock anybody, a flick up and a flick down, freight-first, a man with nothing on his back and no business he could name. His eye went to my empty hands and my worn front and started to slide off, filing me under the yards' whole crowd of tired nobodies. I stopped a little short, close enough to be talked to and not so close as to be looming, and I didn't say his name, because his name spoken by a stranger in this place was the last thing either of us needed loose in the air. I just stood where the fire could reach my face and let him have a second look.

The second look landed different.

I watched it happen slow, which is the only way it happens when the face in front of you is a hundred miles and a whole world out of its place. He looked at me the way you look at a word you know that's been spelled wrong. Something in him said he'd seen the face before, and something else said that couldn't be, not here, not this deep, and the two things sat and argued behind his eyes while the hot thing cooled in his hands. He wasn't frightened. He was just working at it, patient, the same as he'd work at a knot that had gone stiff on him.

Then it came loose.

I saw the knowing go through him, and it didn't come as a shout, nothing down there ever did. It came as a stillness, his whole tired body going quiet over the bowl, and then his mouth working once without sound before it found anything. When it found something it wasn't my name and it wasn't a greeting. It was the plainest question a man can ask, and he asked it low, so it stayed between the two of us and the fire, and the yards went on around it not caring.

"Adz?" he said. Like he was asking me to tell him he was wrong.

I didn't tell him he was wrong. I came the last couple of steps and lowered myself down at the edge of his fire, on his good side, close enough now to talk under everything, and I put my hands out to the flames like a man who'd only come for the warmth, so that anybody glancing over would see two backs sharing a fire and think nothing of it.

"It's me," I said. "Don't get up."

He didn't get up. But he kept looking at me like the looking was the only thing keeping me from turning back into a stranger, and his hands had forgotten the bowl entirely, and I could see him getting his breath under the next thing, the real thing, the what-in-hell-are-you-doing-down-here that was already standing up behind his eyes.

*End of Chapter 47.*
