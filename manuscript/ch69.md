## Chapter 69 — The Wrong Direction

The kitchen has dead hours, same as anywhere. The pot goes quiet, the kid falls asleep sitting up and gets carried off, Lim's hum trails down into actual snoring, and for a while the only thing awake is the stove ticking as it cools. Up top the dead hours are when your number barely moves and you can almost forget you've got one. Down here they're just dark and warm. I'd been home one day and I was already keeping the kitchen's hours like I'd never left, which tells you either how little the month had changed me or how much.

Sol came and sat across from me at the low table. She had two cups of the hot water they drink down here when there's nothing to put in it, and she set one in front of me, which is how I knew it wasn't going to be a short talk. Sol doesn't bring you a drink to say goodnight.

"You've been chewing on it all day," I said.

"So have you. You just do it with your face pointed at the wall so nobody gets to watch."

Fair enough.

"Tell me the machine part," she said. "Not the hope part. I got the hope part this morning and I've been full on it since. Tell me the part you didn't say because Tessie had a spoon in her hand."

So I told her the part.

"A miss isn't the machine letting somebody go. I need to be careful about that, because it's the part that matters and it's the part everybody wants to walk straight past." I turned the cup so I'd have something to do with my hands. "When it wrote Pia, it wrote her for a place. A tier, a unit, a wrist it could reach. The sentence lands on the page it's pointed at. We took Pia off that page — pulled her down here, under the line, where the signal frays and the writing doesn't carry. So the day came, the pen came down, and there was no page under it. That's a miss. Not mercy. A write with nothing to write on."

Sol took that the way she takes things, which is without her face doing much. "So it's about where they are," she said. "Not what they did."

"It's about where the page is. Pia was lucky in a dumb mechanical way, like a letter that doesn't get delivered because the house isn't there anymore." I drank a little of the hot water. It tasted like a cup that had once been near tea. "Same as me. My sentence was written for a man up in the count. By the day it was due I was three weeks into the dark with a dead wrist. Nobody home. The pen hit the floor."

"Then Danny's got a page," she said.

And there it was, out on the table between us, the thing she'd carried down here to say.

"Danny's got a page," I said. "And he's standing right in the middle of it, and the ink's wet on him all day long."

She waited. Sol's good at waiting; it's one of maybe three things we have in common.

"You know how Lim gets a name back sometimes. Malou. A tune. Down here, with the signal gone soft, the old stuff floats up because there's nothing left pressing it down. Danny's the other way around. He's up where the hand never comes off the page. Whatever got cut out of him, the cut's being kept fresh. He isn't a man they finished editing and walked away from. He's a man they're editing right now, every day, so he keeps on not having a sister."

"So get him off the page," she said. "Same as Pia. Same as you."

"That's the trapdoor," I said. "Pia was nine days out and three tiers down, and we reached her through a gap in the machine's attention. Danny's up. Not up like upstairs. Up where the signal's thickest, where the eye never blinks, where a dark wrist gets a hand laid on it and a question asked." I set the cup down flat. "A miss happens going down, Sol. Off the count, under the line. That's the only place the pen ever comes down on nothing. To get Danny a miss, you have to get him down here. And to get him down here, somebody has to go up first and carry him out of the one patch of the world the machine watches hardest. The miss is down. Danny's up. You'd have to walk the whole length of it in the wrong direction to turn him into the kind of lucky we got by accident."

That one took a second to cross her face. It's a slower thing than the hope was. Hope you can get in one gulp. This you have to chew.

"Up top, my whole job used to have a dull cousin," I said, because the quiet had gone on long enough to need a thing thrown in it. "People would delete something and panic and come find me. Easy work. The thing's just sitting in a backup somewhere with the lights off, waiting to be told it still exists. Danny's not deleted. Danny's overwritten, and somebody's leaning on the key."

She didn't laugh. I didn't really expect her to. Some jokes are just there to let the other person know you know how heavy the room is.

"And he wouldn't know me," she said. Quiet. "I've had that part a long time. That one's old."

"I know."

"The new part is you just told me it isn't finished." She wasn't angry. That was the thing that got me — there was nowhere for her to put angry, so she didn't bother making any. "For nine years the reason I don't go up and get my brother is that there's no getting him. He's gone the way a drowned thing is gone, and you build a life with a drowned thing in it. You watch the wall. You make the next name a person before it's a number. And you don't ever cost it out, because there's no sum to do." She wrapped both hands around her cup like the room had gotten colder, which it hadn't. "You just sat there and did the sum."

"I didn't mean to," I said, which was true and useless, the two things I bring to most rooms.

"You did the thing you do. You read it." She turned the cup a quarter around, the way I turn mine. We'd been doing each other's nervous habit for an hour and neither of us was going to mention it. "I asked you to. I know I asked you to."

I'd come down a month of dark to get back to the warm, and I'd been warm for about one evening before I turned back into the one thing I'm genuinely good at, which is finding the wrong row in a table and pointing at it. Tonight I'd pointed at Danny. I'd taken a grave Sol had spent nine years learning to live next to and told her the man in it might not be all the way dead — and then, in the same breath, told her the only road to him ran straight up into the part of the world that had buried him.

A wall you can lean on. A door you can't walk through you just end up standing in front of.

"We're not doing anything tonight," Sol said finally.

"No."

"But it's a door now." She said it like she was testing whether she believed it yet. "This morning he was a wall. Now he's a door."

"Yeah."

"I don't know if that's better."

I didn't know either. I didn't say so. She already knew that was the honest answer, and down here the kindest thing you can do with a true thing is sometimes just let it sit on the table between two cups nobody's drinking.

*End of Chapter 69.*
