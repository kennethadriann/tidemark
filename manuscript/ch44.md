## Chapter 44 — The Cleared Shelf

Something had shifted in the yards by the time I woke, and it took me a while to name it, because it wasn't loud. The racket was the same racket. The fires burned the same. But the lean decider, the one who'd kept four or five backs down the night before when he could have sent them up, still had them. They hadn't gone off to some other fire or some other man's loads. They were his this morning the same as they'd been his in the dark, sitting close, doing nothing, and a thing a man holds onto through a whole night is a thing he means to hold. I'd half wondered in the dark if I'd talked myself into a shape because I wanted one badly enough. But there it was in what passed for daylight, unmoved. The backs were still held. Whatever they were held for hadn't come, and he still thought it would.

Then, some way into the morning by my body's poor clock, he did a thing that took the last of the maybe out of it. He put two of the idle men to clearing the shelf. Not loading. Clearing. They went down the long low stone and moved the odd parcels off it, the slow ordinary loads that had been sitting there waiting on a slow ordinary carry up, and shoved them to one end and left the middle of the shelf bare. A man doesn't clear a landing unless he's making room for a thing about to land. I'd done that kind of housekeeping myself in another life, tidying a table before the big load came in, and I knew it when I saw it. He wasn't keeping the backs down against the chance of a drop anymore. He was sweeping the floor for one. You don't sweep a floor for a thing you only hope is coming.

Once the shelf was clear the feel of the place changed, the way a room changes when someone says the food's nearly ready even if nobody's got up yet. Nothing you could point at. The empty backs waiting to go up drifted, without a word from anyone, to where they could see the shelf, because a man who carries wants to know if there's about to be work worth being early for. The middle of the yards gathered itself toward the cleared stone, and the whole loose weight of the place tipped that way.

That was my chance to move, and I nearly didn't take it, because moving was the one thing I'd spent three days teaching the yards I didn't do. I was the man who kept the poor fire. They knew me for it in the small way they knew anything about me, which is to say only as the fire's keeper. If I got up and walked toward the middle for no reason, I'd be a man with a reason again, and a man with a reason gets a second look.

But the stir had handed me a reason without meaning to. When men drift toward a thing they leave their old warmth behind, and the far side of my fire went thin as the backs that had been warming there wandered off to stand where they could watch the shelf. A fire nobody warms at is a fire nobody's keeping, and a fire down there was worth keeping. There was another poor one off the near end of the shelf, not much of a fire, where a couple of the held men had settled to wait out their being held. It was going down and none of them was the sort to feed it. Feeding it was a thing I could get up and do without a word passing, the same small use I'd bought my first spot with, only this one would put me a good deal closer in.

So I did it the slow way. I didn't march over. I let my own fire sink first, honestly, until there was no sense in sitting at it, and then I got up like a man getting up from a dead fire and not like a man going anywhere, and I went the wood-way, and I dragged a piece to the near-shelf fire, and I fed it, and I put myself down at it. It took me the better part of an hour to cross thirty feet, and I'd have taken longer if the place had been watching me. But the place wasn't watching me. The place was watching the shelf. That was the whole use of the stir, and I'd been waiting on a use like it. When everyone's looking one way a man can go the other way, or the same way slower, and be nothing the whole time he does it.

I stopped where I stopped on purpose. I hadn't crossed into the middle, into where the men who belonged did their belonging, and I didn't mean to. That was the old discipline, the one that had put me at the far edge my first night instead of walking in among them like I had any business there. Getting greedy about the last few feet was how a careful man threw away the careful part. I'd only moved from the back edge to the near one, from a fire at the far wall to a fire at the lip of the thing, and it had cost me nothing but an hour and a dead fire I'd have lost either way.

The held men didn't look at me twice. I was a worn man warming at a poor fire, which was what I'd been at the other one. Only the view was better. From here I could see the lean decider without hunting down a long stretch of yard for him, and I could see the faces of the men he kept, bored and cold and not knowing yet what they were being saved for, which made two of us. I fed the fire when it wanted feeding and I sat where I could watch the cleared stone, closer to where the thing would land than I'd been the day before, and I waited on it the same as they did.

*End of Chapter 44.*
