## Chapter 48 — What in Hell

The question took its time coming, the way a thing does when a man has to get his breath under it first. Maning sat there with his hands off the bowl and looked at me, and I let him look. I'd had weeks to get used to the idea of him. He'd had about a minute to get used to the idea of me, and the idea didn't fit the place, so it took him a while to set it down where it would sit.

When he finally talked it wasn't the big question. He worked up to that the slow way, same as he did everything.

"You're wet," he said.

"Was. Dried out mostly."

"You came the wet way." He said it flat, not a question, turning it over. A man doesn't come the wet way to the yards unless he's a far back with a load or a fool with nowhere better to be, and I was plainly neither the first, so he was left holding the second and not liking the shape of it.

"There a dry way?" I said.

"No." He almost smiled at that, a thing that started at one corner of his mouth and thought better of it. "But there's a less wet way. You'd not know it."

"I'd not."

He picked the bowl back up, mostly to have something to do with his hands, and blew across it without drinking, and kept his eyes on me the whole time like I might dissolve if he looked away and checked back. Then he asked it.

"What in hell are you doing down here, Adz."

There it was. I'd known it was coming since the second he first said my name, and I still didn't have a good answer ready, because the true answer was a slate with his name on it and I wasn't going to put that in the air between the smoke and the hot bowl on the night I found him. So I gave him the other true thing, the small one, and let it stand in for the big one.

"Looking for work," I said.

He looked at me. A long look, the kind that costs the man on the other end of it. Maning had spent his whole life reading the honest weight of things, and he could read a lashing that would hold from one that wouldn't at ten feet in bad light, and he turned that same eye on my four plain words and did not buy them for a second.

"Work," he said.

"There's work down here. You'd know. You're carrying it."

"There's work up top too. Dry work. Clerk's work. Reader's work, the kind a man like you sits down to and never gets his feet wet." He set the bowl on his knee. "You had a tier. You had a number worth having, last I heard it. You don't leave that to come stand at the bottom of the world with the far backs and tell me you're looking for work. Not you. You're the laziest smart man I ever hauled junk for, and a lazy man does not walk to the deepest cold place there is to look for the hardest job there is."

That was a long speech for Maning. It was maybe the longest I'd ever heard out of him at one sitting, and it told me two things. One, he was more shaken than his face let on, because a shaken man talks past his usual ration. Two, he was not going to be handled. I'd half-hoped he'd take the small answer and let the rest wait for a better hour. He wasn't going to.

"It's complicated," I said, which is the thing you say when you're not ready and both of you know it.

"It's always complicated with you." He said it without heat. There was even something in it that was almost fond, the way you're fond of a leak you've patched a dozen times. He looked at me a moment longer, then let it go, not because he was satisfied but because he could see I wasn't going to open the box tonight and he was too tired to pry at it. He'd get at it later. Maning was patient about things the way water was patient about things, which is to say he'd wear you down and be in no hurry doing it. "All right," he said. "Keep your complicated. You eaten?"

"Some. Today."

"That's not eaten." He turned and said a word to the man beside him, low, and money or the memory of money changed hands somewhere, and after a minute a fold of hot leaf came down the fire to me the same as I'd bought myself two nights back, except I hadn't paid for this one. I started to say something about that and he cut it off flat.

"Don't," he said. "You'll owe me. You already owe me. What's one more."

I ate. It was better than the one I'd bought, or I was hungrier, or both. He watched me eat with a kind of grim satisfaction, like feeding me settled something in him that the talking hadn't. Somewhere in there the last of the load-tiredness in his own body seemed to shift over and make room for me being real, and when I looked up he'd stopped watching me like I might vanish and started watching me like a man watches a friend eat, which is a plainer and a warmer thing.

"Pia," he said, when I was near done. Not a question yet. Just laying the name down to see what I'd do with it.

"Safe," I said. "Off the count. She sorts caps still. Tessie's got her." I kept it short and true because that was one I could hand him whole, no complicated on it.

Something went out of his shoulders that I hadn't known was in them. "Good," he said. "That's good." He didn't ask about the rest of them by name, not yet. He was a man who took his news the way he took a load, one manageable piece at a time, testing his footing between each. He'd get to Lim and Sol and the kitchen. Not tonight. Tonight was that Pia sorted caps and I was real and sitting at his fire and he didn't have to understand it yet to be glad of it.

"You look like hell," he told me.

"You look thin."

"I've been out." He said it like that explained everything, which down here it did. Then he settled back on his hands, the tired man finding the ground under him, and looked off across the yards at nothing, at the fires and the far shelf and the dark past all of it. "Long way to come looking for work," he said, mild, not letting it go, just setting it down where I'd have to walk past it again in the morning. "We'll talk in the light."

"We'll talk in the light," I said.

He handed me the last of his own bowl without asking if I wanted it, and I took it without saying I didn't need it, and we sat there, two backs at a middle fire, not talking, while the yards went on being loud and warm and not caring about either of us. It was the first time in a long time I'd sat next to somebody who knew my name and wasn't afraid of it. I ate his cold rice. It was fine.

*End of Chapter 48.*
