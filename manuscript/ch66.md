## Chapter 66 — Word Going Down

So now I had the number, and the number was no good to anybody sitting on my arm.

That's the part nobody warns you about with a thing you went a month to get. You picture the getting. You don't picture the part right after — you at the top of a freight stair, the one man who needs it at the bottom of the world three days of dark away, and you tired in a way that's moved into your bones and started paying rent.

Maning had sent me up to get a number. He'd made my whole come-down argument wait on it — go get your own, he'd said, that's the first thing, like it was a chore, like I'd be back by supper. Well. I had it. It came and it went and I was still here, which was the entire point and the thing he'd want to hear more than his own name. And he was at the yards, and I was not walking back to the yards. I'll get to why. First I had to work out if I could even do the other thing — send it.

Because I'd been here before, and the last time I worked it out, the answer was no.

---

Back when all of this started, when I still thought a problem could be solved by sitting at a table and being clever at it, I'd spent a whole bad day trying to pull Maning down by remote. Send a word up, pull the man down, never leave the warm — my favorite kind of plan, the kind where I don't have to get wet. And every version of it folded the same way. You can't put a man's death-date in a stranger's mouth. You can't move a stubborn man with a vague note. The going can't be sent; only the words can, and words with no legs don't move anybody.

That was true. It's still true. But I'd had it pointed the wrong way round.

I wasn't trying to move Maning this time. That was the whole difference, and it took me a while to see it, because I'm slow about the things that let me off the hook. He'd already decided to decide. The gate wasn't *convince me*; the gate was *bring me the one fact I asked for.* And a fact isn't a going. A fact has legs of its own. "Adz got his number and it had already missed him" — a stranger can carry that the whole way down without ever once understanding what he's holding.

You can't send a man his death. I'd gotten that right the first time. But I could send him a hello. Nobody drops a hello on the trail because it got heavy.

So the plan was back on — delegation, the thing I'm best at in the world. A month of walking so I could get back to the part where I make somebody else do the walking.

---

The trick was the back. It's always the back, never the plan.

I sat at the rim of the lamps and watched the backs come and go off the freight stair. Most were going up, deeper into the tiers, no good to me. I wanted one going down, down the long way, somebody whose road ran through the deep where the far men work. And I wanted one who'd carry a word and not just say he would and then forget it two lamps on. There's a kind of man who drops a message the second it stops being interesting, and a kind who hauls it like it's lashed to his back because somebody trusted him with it. You can usually tell by the hands. Careful hands keep a promise better than an honest face.

I found him on the third cup of watching. An older one, unhurried, coiling a line the slow right way instead of the fast wrong way, going down empty to pick up deep and in no sweat about it.

I went and stood where he'd see me and let him finish before I opened my mouth, which is the whole etiquette down here and about the only manners I've got left.

"You're going down," I said. "Deep? Past the yards?"

"Through them." He didn't stop coiling. "Who's asking."

"Nobody worth the trouble. I've got a word for a man at the yards, if you'll carry it." I held up a metal bit so he could see it wasn't a favor I was begging. "A deep salvager. Big. Works out past everything, stays gone for weeks, careful as a priest with a load. Name's Maning. You'd know him by the way he sets a thing down like it might wake up."

Something moved in his face — not that he knew the man, just that he knew the *type*, which was enough. "A word," he said. "Not a package."

"A word. Three of them, really." I said it plain and slow, the way you give a number you need to come out the other end unbent. "Tell him Adz got it. It came and it went and I'm still here. And tell him I've gone home."

He stopped coiling then and looked at me, and I watched him decide I was either simple or carrying something heavier than it sounded, and settle, like most people do, on simple. Safer that way. "Adz got it, came and went, you're still here, gone home," he said back, flat, filing it. No idea in the world that he'd just repeated a dead man's resurrection to a cold stranger at a lamp. "That's it?"

"That's the whole freight." I put the bit in his hand. "Light load."

"Lightest I've had." He pocketed it and the coil and stood, and that was that; no ceremony, a man taking on a nothing errand on a long walk he was making anyway. He went to the head of the down-stair, and I watched him get smaller, carrying the best thing I had down into the dark toward a friend, and I understood I'd never know for certain it got there.

That's the bill on a sent word. Maning taught it to me himself, with my own line turned back on me: the only way you trust it got there is you carry it. I wasn't going to carry it. I was going to stand here and not know, for good, and let that be the price. A month ago not-knowing would've eaten me alive. Turns out you can learn to pay a thing.

---

And then I did the one part of the whole month I didn't have to be talked into.

I didn't go back down to the yards. Everything I'd packed and carried and waded for was down that way, the man and the fire and the unfinished talk, and the old me would've gone to finish it in person, just to tidy the last row. I stood up and turned the other way instead. Down, but a different down — down through the tiers toward the low water, toward a kitchen under the line where a kid sorts bottle caps, a pot is always on, and nobody has ever once looked at my wrist.

Home. The word for the place, I mean, which I'd been careful not to use for a month because wanting it slows you down. I let myself use it now. I'd sent my word down to one end of the dark, and I pointed myself at the other, and for the first time since the stairwell I was walking toward something instead of after it.

"Alright," I said, to nobody, which is becoming a habit. And I started down.

*End of Chapter 66.*
