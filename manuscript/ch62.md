## Chapter 62 — The Banked Fire

The run went on filling in behind the first lamp, and it filled quicker than I'd have guessed. Coming down, the dark between the far lamps had stretched longer and longer the deeper I got, each new one a smaller reason to keep the feet moving. Going up, the same stretches ran the other way, and the dark between them was shortening. I hadn't gone half so far as I'd gone between lamps down in the deep before the next glow stood up ahead of me, and I marked the shortening without meaning to, the way you mark that a downhill road is easing under you before you've told yourself so.

For a while I could see the one ahead and feel the one behind at the same time, the near dark of them both. In my legs I understood the thing I'd only worked out in my head at the first lamp. The lamps were closing up. The way still had long dark in it, but the long dark was getting shorter by the lamp, and a man climbing it was climbing into country that had more and more reason to keep a light going. I didn't hurry. But I felt the shortening, and I didn't much care for the small part of me that was glad of it.

The second lamp was not like the first. It stood over the same kind of bare widening, with the same fed flame in its bracket and the same cut hollow of water in the cold stone. But off to the side of it, back where the light went thin, somebody had a fire. Not a lamp. A low ring of stones with the coals banked down in it under a gray skin of ash, banked to be found again live. A flat stone was drawn up to sit on, worn pale on top where a man had sat a good many times. A little stack of dry stuff stood by for feeding it. Nobody there.

I stood off and read it before I went near, which was a habit I'd carried up out of the deep and hadn't lost. The banked coals said the man wasn't far, or hadn't been gone long, and meant to come back to a live fire and not start one cold. The worn stone said he came here often and stayed a while, which said the lamp wasn't only a lamp now. It was somebody's spot, a place a tender or some low load-walker kept as his own on a run not yet thick enough for a proper stop. Nothing to buy, nobody to buy it from. Just a man's fire waiting on the man. I held my hand over the banked ash without touching it and it gave back a little warmth, not much. Hours, not the moment. He'd be back, but not while I stood there, if I didn't stand there long.

I drank at the lamp's water and filled what I carried, and then I caught myself at a thing I hadn't done in a long while. I was minding what I left. I'd drawn the water down a finger in the hollow, and I found I wanted it not to show, and I was brushing my own wet prints off the pale stone with my foot, working to leave the spot as I'd found it so the man who kept it would read nothing off me when he got back. Down in the deep I'd left a stranger's crossing-line as I found it too. But that had been a plain honesty, one working man keeping faith with another's hand. This was not that. This was a man rubbing out his own tracks so as not to be read, and I was doing it here without having decided to, because up in this country a fire wasn't only warmth. It was a place a man came back to and stood over and asked, low, who'd been at it while he was gone.

I made myself quit brushing the stone. It was a fool's care and I knew it while I did it. A finger of water gone from a hollow the lamp would fill again, a wet print that'd dry before the tender ever got back, and if he read anything off it at all he'd read that a thirsty man went by, which was the truth and no crime in it. But knowing it was fool's care didn't set the care down, and that was the part worth marking. The count had been working back into me the whole climb. First the counting of days out on the rope, then the line I'd caught my head shaping for a stranger, and now it had got down into my hands, into what they did with a little spilled water while I wasn't watching them. I stood up out of the crouch I hadn't known I'd gone into, and I left the stone wet, and let the wet stay.

The tender didn't come while I was there, and I didn't wait on him. A man keeping a fire on a lonely run would want to know who'd been at his lamp about as much as I wanted to be the stranger he found at it, and there was nothing in the meeting for either of us that a little more walking wouldn't spare us both. I filled my chest with the warm off the lamp one last time, because it was there and free, and I stepped back off the bright stone into the dark of the run. Behind me the two lamps sat in a line now, the first far down and small, the second near and bright, and ahead of me the dark held the third one somewhere, closer than the second had been. I put my feet back on the marked stone and went up toward it.

*End of Chapter 62.*
