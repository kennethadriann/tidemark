## Chapter 59 — Hand Over Hand

The long empty part gave the cold back to me slow, the same as it had taken it, and asked nothing else. Past the split there was no mark worth finding and no light to want, only the up of it and my own breath and the water letting go of me a little more with every stretch I put behind me. I walked it the way you walk a thing you've walked before, not thinking about the far end, because the far end up here was just more of the same until it wasn't. The deep sweat came off the walls and went to honest damp. The low wide smell that had been the air itself down at the yards thinned out to plain wet stone. I was climbing out of the country the water had made, and it was letting me go without a fuss, having never much minded I was in it.

Somewhere along there I came on the cold spot where the far man had kept his fire. I knew it before I was on it, a widening off the way, a black smear on the stone, a bone or two of char gone soft. He'd have been weeks gone from it, off to wherever far men go when the fire's out, and there was nothing of him left but the mark of where he'd sat and let me sit beside him the once. I didn't stop. A cold fire is a poor place to stand. But I put a hand out and touched the stone by it going past, not for any reason I could name, and went on.

Then the way tipped down a short pull and the passage filled up with the deep black standing wet, wall to wall, still as oil, and I was at the crossing.

The line was still there. Fixed horn to horn the way I'd found it, the near hitch the same near hitch, the rope gone a little stiffer with the cold and the wet but holding, a stranger's work still doing its one job in the dark with nobody by to see it done. I stood at the edge and read it the same as I had coming down, and it told me the same thing, that men worked this water and meant to keep working it, that somebody had spent good rope on a crossing he trusted he'd want again. Coming down, that had been the warmest thing I'd found in a week. It had meant people, and people down here meant the far men, and the far men meant Maning at the end of it. A rope in the dark had said, you're getting near.

Going up it said the other thing. It was the last of that. Past this water the deep was behind me for good, and the hands ahead of me weren't the far men's, careless of a wrist, glad enough of one more back. Ahead the hands belonged to the counting country, and the eyes went with them. This rope was the last honest stranger's work I'd lay a hand on before the kind of place I was climbing to. I took the near hitch and tried it with my weight and it held, and I went out over the cold black on it, hand over hand, the same rope going the other way, the wet climbing my sleeves and my legs hunting the footing under a surface I couldn't see. Halfway out the old fear I'd had of this water going down was gone, worn off, and a fresh one had come up in its place.

Because out on the rope, with nothing to do but move my hands and not fall, I caught myself counting. Not the crossing. The days. How many since the yards, since the black water the first time weeks back, my head reaching for a sum the same as it used to reach for one before I'd told it to, laying the count out to see where I stood on it. I got three numbers in before I heard what I was doing. I hadn't counted a day since the long run coming down, when I'd learned there was no keeping them, lamps not being dawns and the dark being all one dark. Down there not counting had come easy. It had come free. Now the country ahead was reaching back down through my own head to switch the counting on again, ahead of a wrist or a lamp, before it had any outside way in at all, and my head had gone along with it like it had never quit.

I got my feet up on the far stone and stood there dripping and let the count fall apart. There was no bottom to it anyway, no first day to start the sum from. But that wasn't why I put it down. I put it down because the counting was the thing itself, the small end of it, the count reaching me by teaching me to keep my own. A man who starts totting up his days has already agreed there's a number, and agreeing there's a number is most of the way to standing where they'll read it back to you. I wasn't there yet. I didn't have to carry it up ahead of me.

I wrung what I could out of my sleeves and left the rest to the cold and looked back the once at the line, still fixed, still holding, doing its work over the black for the next man down. Then I turned my face up the way and climbed, and the water behind me went back to being still, and I was on the near side of the last of the deep, going up into the part that counted.

*End of Chapter 59.*
