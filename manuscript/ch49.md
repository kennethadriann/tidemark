## Chapter 49 — In the Light

Morning at the yards wasn't a light. It was a change in the noise.

There's no sky down there to go gray and no sun to come up over anything, so what comes up instead is the work. Sometime in the dark the fires got fed higher, and the lumps under the blankets turned back into men, and the deciders took up their still places along the shelf, and the whole widening went from the sound of a place sleeping to the sound of a place with things to move. That was the light Maning had meant. I woke into it stiff on one hip, the fire down to a red nothing, and found him already sitting up, working his hands open and shut, taking the measure of the day like a man tests a rope before he trusts weight to it.

"You snore," he said, without turning around.

"I don't."

"You do. Like a pump with something in it." He said it flat, but it was the nearest thing to a good morning I was going to get, and it was more than anybody had handed me in weeks, so I took it and didn't argue the pump.

He didn't come at me right off, and I knew better than to be glad of it. A man who tells you at night he'll get to a thing in the light, and then doesn't reach for it the second his eyes open, hasn't let it go. He means to do it properly and he's in no hurry, and those are the worst kind to wait on.

So we had a while of nothing much first. He drank his water and grumbled at his knees, which had their own opinions about the cold he'd slept in. He told me a little about the drop he'd hauled up the wet way, not because it mattered but because it was a safe thing to have in our mouths, and both of us knew we were standing on it a minute before the ground got worse. Good junk, he said. Deep junk, the kind that paid. He'd been further out than he liked, longer than he liked, to get it. I said it had looked heavy. He said everything worth the trip was.

Then he set his cup down, and he looked at me, and the standing on safe things was done.

"All right," he said. "It's light."

"It's light," I agreed, and didn't help him any further than that.

"Last night you told me you came all this way looking for work." He wasn't smiling now, and he wasn't hard either. He was level, a man laying a thing on the ground between us so we could both look at it. "I let it sit overnight to see would it look better by morning. It looks worse. A man lies to you tired, that can be the tired talking. A man lies to you rested, he means it. I'd rather you didn't mean it."

"I'm not lying," I said, which was true and no help at all, because there's a lot of country between not lying to a man and telling him the thing.

"No," he said, slow, reading me the whole time. "No, I don't believe you are. That's what's got me sat here."

He turned his cup over in his hands and worked at it, and I let him, because a man who arrives at a thing on his own two feet stands on it steadier than one you carry there. And Maning was most of the way to it already. He'd been most of the way since the fire the night before. He just hadn't wanted to walk the last of it in the dark.

"You didn't come for work," he said. "And you didn't come for the scenery, there being none. You came down the wet way, which no man in his right mind does, past every dry job between your tier and here, to the one set of yards a body could stand in and wait for me to come up out of the water. That's weeks of walking. You'd have had to set out not long after I did." He looked up from the cup. "You came for me."

I could have told him no. I'd told smaller lies to bigger men for less than was riding on this one. But it was Maning, and he'd said it plain and true with his eyes on me, and the no would not have come out of my mouth in any shape he'd have bought. There was no sense spending it.

"Yeah," I said. "I came for you."

He nodded, once, like a man who'd known the answer and only wanted it out of the other fellow's mouth to be sure of it. It didn't gladden him. A friend walking to the bottom of the world after you is not good news even when it's a friend you're glad to see. It only means there's a reason, and the reason is never that all's well.

"For me," he said again, quiet, fitting the thing to himself, and I watched him not care for how it fit. He set the cup down between his boots and put his forearms on his knees and looked into the coals a good while. When he spoke again he didn't grab for it. He asked it easy, almost gentle, which was worse than if he'd taken me by the collar.

"So what's wrong, Adz."

And that was the morning, and the light, and no wet way and no dry way around it that I could find, and I'd been looking for one since the kitchen. I set my own cup down so my hands would have nothing to argue with. Then I told him.

*End of Chapter 49.*
