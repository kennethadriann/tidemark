## Chapter 71 — The Man Who Came Down

Think fast, Sol said. Doors don't stay ajar. Here's the honest version of thinking fast: I did it for the better part of two weeks and arrived nowhere, which is about my career average.

The days went by the way days go by down here, which is to say without a number on them. Up top you always know the date, because the date is stapled to your water. Down in the kitchen you lose it. You eat when the pot's hot, you sleep when the stove ticks cool, and one morning you count back and find a couple of weeks have gone somewhere and you couldn't swear to how. I'd been home long enough that home had started to mean this.

And the Danny question just sat there. The kitchen's good at that. Not hiding a thing — swallowing it, the way a pond takes a stone. The stone's still down there. You just stop seeing it once the ripples quit. So I wrung out socks that weren't mine and stirred a pot that didn't need it and read the wall at night next to Sol, and neither of us said the name. I'm very good at not saying a name. It might be the one athletic thing I do.

Then one evening the water changed, and Pia heard it first.

She was at her caps, sorting them by the system only she's ever understood, and her head came up before the rest of us felt anything. Kids down here read the water the way I read a file — some part of them is always half-listening to it. A second later we all got what she'd got: the wade and drip of somebody coming up the shallow way, under the line, in no particular hurry about it.

Nobody reached for anything. That's the kitchen too. Up top a sound you don't expect is a reason to find the exits. Down here Tessie just looked at the dark mouth of the passage and waited to see what the water had brought her, the same way she'd look at a pot to see if it was ready.

What it brought her was a man wet to the waist with a load still on his back.

I knew the walk before I let myself know the man. That's the order it comes in, if you've spent any time reading bodies instead of faces — the freight first, then the person under it. A careful tested step, a roll of the shoulder to shift a weight he'd been beneath too long, and the particular tiredness of a man who'd walked a long way on purpose. He'd come down loaded. Of course he had. You could tell him the house was on fire and he'd still look around for something worth carrying out of it.

"You're dripping on my floor," Tessie said.

"It's water," Maning said. "Floor's already wet. I checked." He let the load down off his back, slow, the way he lets everything down, and stood up out from under it and worked the ache out of his shoulders, and then he found me across the room.

I want to tell you he looked surprised. He didn't. Maning doesn't do surprised; he does a slow recalculation, like a man retotaling a column he thought he'd already closed. His eyes went down me once — dry clothes, no load, a month of the yards worn off me — and he set the sum where he could see it.

"Huh," he said. "You made it home."

"I made it home."

"Word did too. Some back handed me three lines off you at the yards. Came and went, still here, gone home." He said it flat, the way he'd recite a load's weight. "Took me a while to work out what to do with it."

"And you settled on getting wet."

"I settled on this. A man tells me I'm dead, then goes and isn't, himself — that's a man worth coming to look at in person." He lowered himself onto the bench by the stove like his knees had opinions, which they do. "Figured if the dying's gone optional down here, I'd come see where they keep the option."

So there it was, the whole of why he came, and also none of it, which is how Maning hands you a thing. I'd walked a month up into the dark to fetch this man, talked myself hoarse at his fire, and left without a yes. And here he was on his own two feet, carrying his own load, on his own slow read of me, weeks after I'd given up. I should have it by now and I keep having to learn it over: you can't carry a man down. You leave the door open and go home and hope his feet work it out behind you.

Tessie put a bowl in his hands before he'd finished sitting. He looked at it, then at her, and something in his face let go a notch.

"You remembered I like it burnt on the bottom," he said.

"I didn't remember anything. That's just where the good part is."

He ate. The room went back to its evening around him — Pia to her caps, Lim humming low, the stove ticking — and a man come up out of the water folded into the night like he'd always had a seat there. That's the kitchen's trick. It doesn't make a thing of you. It just makes you a place.

I sat and watched him work through his burnt lugaw, and I was glad. I genuinely was. Right up until the other thing arrived underneath the glad, quiet and fully formed, the way the heavy ones always get to me a half-second late.

Here was Maning. A man dated out, come down off the count to be a warm dead man in a kitchen — one more name the pen would reach for and find no page under. Good. That was the whole of what I'd offered him, and he'd taken it. I should've been able to just leave it there, a thing that worked out.

But Maning wasn't only a body by the stove. Maning was the one man I'd ever met who knew the up-country in his feet — the long run and the thick signal, the ways the far men walk where the eye never blinks. He read that ground for a living. And the one live want in this room, the one we'd both quit saying out loud, pointed straight up into exactly that country.

Two weeks I'd leaned on a single fact to keep from looking at the door: the up-road to Danny ran through one patch of nobody, and the nobody was me, the last Reader in the kitchen, dragging my feet in front of the exit marked out. Only Adz can read it, and Adz doesn't want to. That had been the whole wall I got to hide behind.

And now there was a man at the stove who could walk it.

I didn't say any of that. He'd been here twenty minutes and didn't even know Danny was a word yet. I just watched the room get quietly more able to do the hard thing, and I ate my own bowl, and I kept not saying the name.

It was fine. The lugaw was good. The door was still open.

*End of Chapter 71.*
