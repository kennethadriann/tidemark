## Chapter 52 — Two Dark Wrists

He didn't think on it fast, and he didn't do it where I could watch.

The first thing he did was go back to work. A decider came along the fires looking for a back that knew a heavy drop, and Maning got up and went, because a man dated out still has a load he came in with and a bowl to earn against it, and because I think he wanted his hands on something that wasn't the thing I'd set down in front of him. I let him go. Waiting was the one piece of the whole business I was any good at, and I'd had a lot of practice at it lately. I fed the fire and ate what the last of my metal would buy and watched the yards go about itself, and I did not once drift down toward the shelf to see how a man looked while he was thinking about the day he'd die.

He came back when the yards had gone from loud to low, tired in the way deep work leaves a man, gray around the mouth. He lowered himself down across the fire and worked a boot off and turned it over and knocked it out against the stone, and he didn't say anything about any of it, and neither did I. We ate. Somebody two fires over was singing something with no tune to it, badly, and stopping, and starting again.

"I did my thinking," he said, after a while.

I didn't jump at it. "All right."

"Not all of it. Some." He set the boot down. "You want to hear where I got to."

"When you want to tell me."

"Now, or I'd not have said." He looked at me across the low flame, and there was something in it I hadn't seen off him yet, not the night before and not that morning. He'd stopped weighing his own end. He was weighing me. "I've been out my whole life reading what a load costs before I get under it. A man doesn't last out past everything if he can't look at a thing and know what it'll take out of him to carry. And I've been sitting here half a day doing it to you instead of to the drop."

"That's a poor use of a day."

"Be quiet a minute." He said it without heat. "You walked three weeks. Down the wet, past every dry job a man could stop and take, into the coldest deepest place there is, to stand at my fire and tell me I'm dated out and try to talk me down under the sea. And the whole time you've kept that arm tucked in easy at your side so nobody reads the wrist. Dark. Same as mine."

I didn't say anything. I knew where he was walking and I couldn't find the mark to leave that would turn him off it.

"You didn't come up here out of the goodness of your heart," he said. "You've not got that much goodness in you, and I mean that kind. You came up into the count, where it can see you fine, to fetch a man out. A lazy man. A careful one. You don't spend three weeks walking into the very thing you're running from, for a friend, not unless." He let it sit. "So. You've got one too."

There it was, laid on the stone between us, and I found I'd been keeping my own arm easy at my side while he said it, the same small hiding that had come up the whole line with me and was still going on by itself.

"Yeah," I said. "I've got one too."

"Dated when."

And that was the thing. I didn't have the number to give him. I'd had it once, clean, a number that fit on a working wrist, back when the wrist still told me things. Then I'd gone under the line and it had gone dark with everything else, and somewhere in three weeks of walking toward his date I had quietly stopped keeping my own. I couldn't have told him that night how much of it was left. I'd made a whole trade out of not looking.

"I don't know," I said. "It went dark when I went off the count. I stopped counting it."

He took that in slow. "You stopped counting your own."

"I had yours to count."

"Huh," he said. The same small sound as the morning before, a man setting a second weight down beside the first to see if the two of them balanced. He looked at my arm, and then at his own, the two dark wrists, and I watched the shape of the thing move around behind his eyes, the shape of what I'd walked up here to be.

"That changes it," he said.

"Changes what."

"The size of what you're asking. And who's doing the asking." He didn't finish it the way I'd braced for. He didn't hand me a rescued man's thanks and he didn't hand me a settled man's no. He picked the boot back up, and turned it, and looked at nothing in particular. "A man came to fetch me out with his own name on the same wall. That's a different thing than a friend feeling sorry for me. I'll want to know that number, Adz. Before I say anything either way. I'll want to know the thing you stopped looking at."

"I don't have it to give you."

"Then that's the first thing to go and get," he said, "isn't it," and he pulled the boot back on, and that was as far as either of us took it that night.

*End of Chapter 52.*
