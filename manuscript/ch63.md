## Chapter 63 — The One Who Dropped Nothing

It took three more days to climb back to the top stop, and I'm not going to pretend I remember them clean. Here's the honest version.

The lamps closed up the way the climb had been promising. Then they stopped being lamps over nothing and started being lamps over somebody — a pot under one, a face by the pot, a man selling cups of warm water like it was a trade and not a mercy. I paid where I had to and kept my head down where I could. I ate what I could afford, which wasn't much, and I was hungry the rest of the time in the flat ongoing way you quit arguing with. Hunger's like a bad coworker. You don't beat it. You learn to sit next to it and get your work done.

I slept twice in bends off the light, the sack under my head, the dark wrist tucked where a tired man tucks a cold hand. Nobody bothered me. A back going up a marked way is the most boring thing a marked way has, and boring was the one thing I'd always been good at. I'd spent twenty-nine years perfecting it. Finally paying off, out here, where there was nobody to be impressed.

Then the run tipped. It had climbed for days and then it eased, and then it dropped a little, and the dropping was wrong enough to stop me until I remembered — right, the top stop sits low to the long run, I'd gone up out of it the other way. The dark ahead went warm at its edges. There was smoke in it, and noise, the particular noise a lot of people make when none of it is aimed at you. I came down the last of the slope and the lit field opened up, the same as the day I'd first dropped a load in it. A cave that had decided to be a town and was still committed to the idea.

I'd earned something here once. I'd come back to spend it.

---

The receiving heap was where I'd left it, a slope of landed loads with the tally-man at the top of it on his stool, slate on his knee, the stillest man in a place full of moving ones. I knew the stillness. I'd clocked it the first time as the kind I used to have, back when my whole job was deciding which row to cut and which to keep. I went up and stood where he'd see me, and waited. You don't walk up on a still man down here. You let him finish being still and notice you on his own time.

He looked at me the way you look at a word you've seen before and can't place. Then he placed it.

"You came back down," he said.

"Came back up," I said. "Mostly."

"From the yards." Not a question. He'd read the wet off me weeks ago; now he read the long dry of the climb back. "You're the one dropped nothing."

I'd honestly forgotten I was anybody. It landed strange, being remembered for the one clean thing I'd done in a month of barely doing anything at all. A whole reputation, built on not screwing up a single load.

"That's me," I said. "The man who dropped nothing. Carve it on my stone."

He almost smiled. Down here that's a standing ovation. "Light load," he said. "Don't go carving too much."

"I'll take what I can get."

He let me stand there a minute, which was his version of offering me a chair. I used it. "I'm going on up," I said. "Toward the tiers. What's the way these days."

He was quiet a second — the quiet of a man you've asked the price of something, deciding whether to quote you the real one. "The way's the same," he said. "The getting onto it changed." He tipped his chin at the far side of the field, the up-end, where the lamps bunched toward a single bright mouth. There was a knot of people over there that didn't move like the rest of the room. It had a shape. People do that when they're in a line and don't want to admit it's a line.

"They put a man on the mouth," he said. "Since the last count went funny. He reads wrists before he lets you up."

"Reads them."

"Takes your arm. Looks at your number. Good number, you go up. No number—" He turned a hand over, flat, the way you show a thing's empty. "No number, you don't go up. You go sit against that wall till somebody comes down from above who wants to know why a grown man's walking around with nothing on his arm." He said it like a weather report. Cold front moving in, bring a jacket. "Didn't used to be. Used to be you went up if your legs could take you up."

Then he looked at my sleeve. At the hand I had laid easy over the cuff without having told it to get there.

"Yours working?"

I thought about lying. I'm good at it on paper and useless at it out loud; something in my face always files the correction before my mouth can finish. "No," I said.

"Then you don't go up the easy way." He said it without any weight on it, which was worse than if he'd leaned. He went back to his slate. Not unkind. Just done. He'd answered the question I asked and had no answer for the one underneath it, and he wasn't going to waste either of us pretending otherwise.

---

I thanked him and took myself off to the cold edge of the field, the same lip of dark I'd rested on the first time, and I sat and let it settle in.

Here's what I'd gotten wrong. I'd spent a month learning to be nobody. A dark wrist was nothing in the deep. A worn man at a poor fire drew no eye. I'd gotten so good at not being read that I'd started to think of it as a thing I owned, a skill, the way I used to think of Tuko humming along in the walls doing my job for me. And a skill is only worth what the room will pay for it. The deep paid well. This field, when I'd dropped my load in it, had paid in the one coin I needed — nobody looking.

The field had learned to look while I was gone.

And the funny part, if you've got the stomach for it, is that it had learned to look for exactly me. Not Adz. Not a face. A wrist with no number on it, walking up toward the count on its own two feet. I'd made myself into the precise thing the new man at the mouth was posted there to catch. I'd filed myself as unreadable and the system had added a column.

Up there in the thick country my own number was sitting in my own file, whole, waiting. The one copy that could tell me whether the sentence had landed or missed. To get to it I had to walk up through a mouth where a man took your arm and read it — and the reading of it was the exact thing I'd come all this way to not do.

I didn't have the next move yet. That was fine. I'm not a next-move man; I'm a sit-with-it man, and I'd just been handed a thing worth sitting with. So I sat at the edge of the lights where nobody read me, for a little while longer, while I still could.

*End of Chapter 63.*
